# Inkling 2 Mini ties Qwen: test, don't switch

As of 27 November 2026, Thinking Machines Lab's Inkling 2 Mini and Alibaba's Qwen3.8-27B are a **dead heat on the most neutral scoreboard available: 20 vs 20 on Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index v5.0.2** ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/inkling-2-mini-vs-qwen3-8-27b)). Underneath the tie the two models win different jobs. Inkling 2 Mini is far cheaper and faster on hosted APIs, and its lab reports leads in agentic coding, search and long context, but nobody has independently replicated those coding wins. Qwen leads on knowledge-work deliverables, factual reliability, human-preference writing votes, multi-turn chat and tool-call reliability. Both labs' launch materials are accurate but selective. Qwen compares itself with an older Claude model on benchmarks it partly built and "corrected". Thinking Machines publishes no evaluation settings at all, and its own text contradicts its own table on instruction following. For the narrator, whose product runs Qwen3.8-27B in FP8 with thinking on, on a rented GPU, for long-form writing and planning jobs that return JSON, **the answer is not to switch now**. His workload sits on Qwen's side of the split. Inkling's speed edge shrinks on datacenter GPUs, and its serving stack is one week old. His own client code also leaves the two biggest levers inside Qwen unused: reasoning effort and enforced structured output. The honest video is a story about a tie and what each side's small print hides, not a coronation.

## Both launch tables are true, and both are curated

Alibaba's pitch is that a 27B dense model "matches the performance of Qwen3.7-plus — an MoE model ten times its size" ([Alibaba Cloud Community, 17 Aug 2026](https://www.alibabacloud.com/blog/alibaba-unveils-qwen3-8-27b_603463)). Its own table only roughly supports that. The 27B leads Qwen3.7-Plus on most coding and agent rows but trails it on **GPQA Diamond (89.2 vs 90.3), HLE (30.8 vs 34.7)** and several vision rows. "Ten times its size" holds for total parameters (397B) but not active ones: Qwen3.7-Plus activates 17B against the 27B's 27B ([HF Qwen3.8-Flash-Next card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next)). The frontier comparison column is "Opus4.6 Max", an older Anthropic model, although Qwen's own Max blog from the same month already compares against newer ones ([Qwen3.8-Max blog](https://qwen.ai/blog?id=qwen3.8)). The footnotes carry the rest. Coding scores were mostly run by Qwen in the Claude Code harness at temperature 1.0 with a 256K window. Its SWE-bench Pro is a modified variant where "problematic tasks were corrected, and all baseline models were re-evaluated on the refined benchmark". QwenSWEBench and CoWorkBench are in-house. The card never states which reasoning effort produced its scores, though the default is `xhigh` ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B)). There is no dedicated blog post or technical report for the 27B. The only qwen.ai post covers Qwen3.8-Max. Alibaba makes no specific claim about long-form writing or JSON reliability. The closest is **IFBench 79.5**, on which the 27B leads every model in its own table ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B)).

Thinking Machines' pitch is "a fast, powerful model that's small enough to run on your laptop" that "leads every open-weights model with less than 100B total parameters on benchmarks such as DeepSWE v1.1 (48.2%) and Vals Index v2.2 (46.5%)" ([Thinking Machines, 20 Nov 2026](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/)). Its small print is thinner than Qwen's. The launch post and both model cards carry **no statement of effort level, temperature, number of runs, or who produced the competitor scores**. The only caveat is a chart caption: "Evaluation setups vary by model." That is notable because the lab's July and early-November posts did state such settings ([Inkling launch, Jul 2026](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/introducing-inkling/); [Inkling 2 Small](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-small/)). The Vals Index headline has **no Qwen3.8-27B score in the same row**, so "leads" is uncontested there. The limitations paragraph says Mini is "behind Qwen3.8-27B" on ComplexConstraints v1.1 at 33.4%, yet the lab's own table lists **Mini 33.4% vs Qwen 30.1%** ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/)). The DeepSWE effort-sweep chart on the same page peaks at 44.6%, below the 48.2% headline. The "4.8× faster" speed chart (54.4 vs 11.4 tokens/s) is a **theoretical memory-bandwidth ceiling "at 614 GB/s with BF16 weights and batch size one"**, not a measurement. The lab's Qwen figures for Terminal Bench 2.1 (73.0) and DeepSWE (42.2) match Alibaba's self-reported numbers exactly, even though the harness labels differ. That suggests at least some rival scores were copied rather than re-run ([HF Inkling card](https://huggingface.co/thinkingmachines/Inkling-2-Mini)). Fairness also requires noting that the lab's less-publicised website model card adds about 25 rows. Many of them are Qwen wins, including **GPQA Diamond 90.5 vs 86.6, HLE text-only 33.9 vs 30.7 and Tax Agent Bench 30.9 vs 14.1** ([TML website model card](https://thinkingmachines.ai/model-card/inkling-2-mini/)).

The parameter count needs a careful caption. Inkling 2 Mini is a 26.4B-total mixture-of-experts model with **3.4B active parameters, but because its middle 12 of 24 layers run three times per token, it has 5.4B "effective" active parameters** ([HF Inkling card](https://huggingface.co/thinkingmachines/Inkling-2-Mini)). The lab is unusually candid in its limitations. It says looping "doesn't enable its weights to store more facts", that the model "sometimes became stuck in long reasoning loops", that it "may take destructive actions", and that "setting reasoning effort to its minimum does not fully disable reasoning" ([HF Inkling card](https://huggingface.co/thinkingmachines/Inkling-2-Mini)). Alibaba's 27B card has no limitations section. Its admissions are buried in usage notes about repetition, language mixing and static-YaRN degradation on short texts ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B)).

## Independent referees score a tie with a split personality

Artificial Analysis gives the cleanest head-to-head, and it confirms the tie. Both models score 20, with Qwen at its default `xhigh` effort ([AA comparison](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/inkling-2-mini-vs-qwen3-8-27b)). The sub-scores split along a clear line. Qwen leads the knowledge-work tests that most resemble document deliverables: **AA-Briefcase 1391 vs 1263, AutomationBench 48.2% vs 41.8%, and Strategy & Ops 39 vs 35**. Inkling leads science reasoning (CritPt 17.0% vs 4.0%) and edges FrontierCode. On factuality, Inkling knows slightly more (accuracy 20.5% vs 17.3%) but makes things up more often: its **non-hallucination rate is 63.4% against Qwen's 71.4%** ([OpenRouter, AA data for Inkling](https://openrouter.ai/thinkingmachines/inkling-2-mini); [OpenRouter, AA data for Qwen](https://openrouter.ai/qwen/qwen3.8-27b)). The headline cost and speed gap is real but measures endpoints, not models. AA ran Inkling on Thinking Machines' API and Qwen on Alibaba's slow, expensive one, giving **$0.59 vs $3.53 per index task and 175 vs 59 tokens/s**. AA also flags Inkling 2 Mini as **the most verbose model in its class, at 161M output tokens on the index against Qwen's 128M** ([AA Inkling page](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/inkling-2-mini)). That directly undercuts the lab's line that looping lets the model "reach the right answer with fewer tokens".

Other independent sources mostly lean towards Qwen, and several walk back the lab's numbers. Vals' own launch note gave Inkling 46.46% on its index. The live page now shows **44.03%, ranked 38th of 43**, and Inkling's Vibe Code Bench score has fallen from 77.92% to **58.00% on the newer v1.1.1**. On that version Qwen's older 64.85% now leads ([Vals: Inkling 2 Mini](https://www.vals.ai/models/thinkingmachines_inkling-2-mini)). Vals also measured **Qwen at 86.00% on SWE-bench Verified with a bash-only harness, about 8 points above the 77.8% that Thinking Machines printed** for its rival ([Vals SWE-bench](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/swebench)). On Vals benchmarks both models ran, Inkling wins cyber and code migration, and Qwen wins legal research (36.06% vs 17.79%), tax and competitive programming ([Vals: Qwen 3.8 27B](https://www.vals.ai/models/alibaba_qwen3.8-27b)). LMArena voters prefer Qwen across text, instructions, hard prompts and multi-turn chat ([BenchClub](https://www.benchclub.ai/compare?m=qwen-qwen3.8-27b&m=thinkingmachines-inkling-2-mini)). Inkling's creative-writing score rests on only 423 votes, and Inkling 2 Mini has no EQ-Bench long-form entry yet ([EQ-Bench](https://eqbench.com/creative_writing_longform.html)). On the hosted market, OpenRouter's weighted-average output price actually paid is **$0.2953/M for Inkling vs $2.409/M for Qwen**, roughly 8× cheaper ([OpenRouter: Inkling](https://openrouter.ai/thinkingmachines/inkling-2-mini); [OpenRouter: Qwen](https://openrouter.ai/qwen/qwen3.8-27b)).

Local speed depends on the GPU, which matters for the narrator. When Qwen cannot fit in VRAM, Inkling wins enormously: **38.2 vs 6.1 tokens/s on a 16 GB RTX 5060 Ti** ([CyberQ](https://cyberq.tw/2026/11/21/inkling-2-mini-debuts-5b-enables-a-loop/)). When both fit, the gap nearly disappears: **38.7 vs 34.5 tokens/s on an RTX 3090 with the same llama.cpp build, and Qwen read prompts faster** ([Bench Lab](https://benchlab.ironyaditya.xyz/posts/2026-11-22-inkling-2-mini-vs-qwen-27b-pagoda/)). On datacenter-class hardware, one disclosed-interest tester measured **Qwen FP8 with its built-in multi-token-prediction draft on an H200 at 189 tokens/s single-stream and 1,070 aggregate at 8 concurrent requests**. In the same tester's results, Inkling on an RTX 5090 reached 161 and 740 ([dev.to, Blixild](https://dev.to/jacek_garbuliski/inkling-2-mini-vs-qwen38-27b-and-arete-27b-on-agent-tasks-54ii)). The loop costs speed as well. Running Inkling at one pass instead of three doubled decode speed but more than doubled perplexity ([Destroy / Rebuild](https://www.destroyrebuild.xyz/blog/inkling-on-mac/)).

## YouTube sells a knockout while stopwatches record a draw

The highest-reach videos sell a knockout. Bijan Bowen's "Inkling 2 Mini Is INSANE" (about 168K views) and WorldofAI's "BEATS Qwen 3.8 27B" (about 97K) are one-shot visual builds judged by eye. Bowen admits on camera that he "cheated a little because I'm just using it from their API" ([Bijan Bowen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OdMpNiByntM); [WorldofAI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cUuVObUqbn0)). WorldofAI repeats the lab's "uses less tokens" claim, which AA's data contradicts. The same two channels ran the same template for Qwen in August ("Basically Opus Locally"), so the "new local king" frame is recycled ([WorldofAI, Aug](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J_aqblUWj4k)). Channels that ran repeated, timed tests describe a tie. The Dark Factory's 136-job test scored **Inkling 123 vs Qwen 116**, "as good as people say, but only by a little" ([The Dark Factory](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cAZnPv8UgjY)). Luke's Dev Lab called the two "almost… interchangeable" ([Luke's Dev Lab](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XR54-ACn6tI)). AI Coding Daily found Inkling near the bottom of its leaderboard at medium effort and level with Qwen only at max effort, "a little bit overhyped on Twitter" ([AI Coding Daily](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XKISwJQJwws)). DIY Smart Code went the other way on a 24 GB AMD card: **Inkling's nine runs took 2 hours 40 minutes against Qwen's 21 minutes, and Inkling wrote 141,000 tokens to Qwen's 66,000** for one fewer pass ([DIY Smart Code](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=av9e098JVOs)).

The flight-simulator demo, the launch's signature image, reproduces only in a narrow form. The official run used a 48 GB M5 Pro MacBook and was played mostly at 15× speed ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/)). JeredBlu got a basic 3D flight game in 11 minutes on a 36 GB Mac in one harness. In another, the model "ran for over an hour… and it never wrote a single line of code", with 20 GB of swap ([JeredBlu](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DCcPlV2iHzw)). Samuel Gregory capped a Mac to about 21 GB of GPU memory to mimic a 32 GB machine. He reported that "Inkling crashed every time" on the longest job while Qwen got through, so "Quen looks like the safer bet" at that size ([Samuel Gregory](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H_sjmoqmzO8)).

Real users converge on a few consistent themes. Both models **overthink at their shipped defaults**, and both communities apply the same fix of turning effort down. Simon Willison's Qwen pelican "took 21 minutes to generate, using 22,276 reasoning tokens to produce 3,223 tokens of output" ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/aug/16/qwen-38-27b/)). Inkling users report a 19,200-token cap hit, a 12,000-token loop and a three-hour, 180-tool-call runaway ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50581260)). Inkling's distinctive complaints match its lab's admissions: it invents facts and then defends them (a made-up WordPress hook), and it is stubborn and literal. Qwen's distinctive complaint is over-reach, summed up in "a damn good coder, but a terrible collaborator" that turns a two-line change into "a 100 line linter style mess" ([r/LocalLLaMA](https://www.reddit.com/r/LocalLLaMA/comments/1w4wjxd/everyone_is_ts_maxing_38_but_after_a_week_of/)). Much of Inkling's first-week trouble is plumbing rather than the model. Early GGUFs gave about 130 perplexity until a loop-slot fix, a llama.cpp grammar bug crashed tool calls, MLX had no tool parser, and builds that fell back to the CPU ran at about 10 tokens/s ([Ollama #19826](https://github.com/ollama/ollama/issues/19826); [llama.cpp #32818](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/32818)). A reviewer on a 7900 XTX went from about 26 to "80-100 tok/s" in four days and told readers to "chalk all the slowness talk above up to model newness" ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50581260)). Qwen's parser bugs are older and fixed in current vLLM and SGLang releases ([vLLM #54808](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/54808); [SGLang #35148](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/issues/35148)).

## The narrator's cheapest upgrade is inside Qwen

The narrator's workload maps onto the split above, and most of it falls on Qwen's side. His product's client ([`qwen38.py`](../../../backend/app/providers/qwen38.py)) sends every request to `Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B-FP8` with `"thinking": True` and a 49,152-token cap that includes reasoning. It records a measured speed of about **70 tokens/s, so a full-length answer takes about 12 minutes**. It also keeps only the messages and **drops both `response_format` and `reasoning_effort`**. Unless the GPU server sets an effort itself, his jobs run at Qwen's default `xhigh`, the setting Willison called "absolutely not a good way to run the model" ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/aug/16/qwen-38-27b/)). JSON is enforced by prompt instructions and an after-the-fact check, with one retry ([`DATABASE_STRUCTURE.md`](../../../DATABASE_STRUCTURE.md)). It is not enforced by the serving engine's structured decoding, which works for Qwen on current vLLM ([vLLM #54808](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/54808)). The client also refuses prompts over 45,000 characters, roughly 11,000 tokens. That makes **Inkling's 1M-token context and long-context benchmark leads irrelevant to his current jobs**.

On each dimension his jobs care about, the evidence leans the same way. For planning, the knowledge-work evidence favours Qwen (AA-Briefcase, Strategy & Ops). The single most relevant anecdote also favours Qwen: on a ticket with contradictory rules, "Qwen flagged it after about 4,000 tokens. Inkling thought for 32,000 tokens, then quietly picked one rule" ([The Dark Factory](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cAZnPv8UgjY)). For complex JSON schemas, Thinking Machines itself says Mini is "less reliable than larger models at following detailed instructions" and weaker on multi-constraint prompts ([HF Inkling card](https://huggingface.co/thinkingmachines/Inkling-2-Mini)). Small tests show both models producing valid strict JSON at low effort ([kingy.ai](https://kingy.ai/blog/inkling-2-mini/); [AICodeKing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JFoMJ17MOOA)). For long-form prose, Qwen has months of reports describing it as a strong, rule-following structured writer whose fiction can feel "mechanical" ([r/SillyTavernAI](https://www.reddit.com/r/SillyTavernAI/comments/1wpwwxg/my_impressions_on_current_highend_local_models/)). Inkling 2 Mini has essentially no long-form writing evidence. The one launch-week creative-writing sample on YouTube drew "a bunch of word salad that has no real point" from Bijan Bowen, the model's most enthusiastic reviewer ([Bijan Bowen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OdMpNiByntM)). For factual content, Qwen hallucinates less.

On a rented GPU, cost is wall-clock time per job, so the question is whether Inkling finishes his jobs faster at equal quality. The closest test to his workload is second-hand: Nick Greece's 2,031 business classification jobs, relayed by the YouTuber Kai. **With thinking on, Inkling wrote about 1,200 tokens per job to Qwen's 2,500 and completed 479 jobs an hour to Qwen's 359, but Qwen was more accurate (86.6% vs 83%)** ([Kai](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t6OlkpO4leM)). That trade, about a third more throughput for a few points of accuracy, is plausible for his pipeline, but it rests on one unverified relay. On the other side, AA's index runs and two independent tests show Inkling writing anywhere from about a quarter more tokens than Qwen to more than double ([AA](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/inkling-2-mini); [novcog test](https://inkling2mini.novcog.us.com/our-test); [DIY Smart Code](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=av9e098JVOs)). Its native NVFP4 weights get hardware acceleration only on Blackwell-class GPUs. One tester also found the published vLLM image "lacks the code for H100 and H200 cards", against a merged vLLM change claiming Hopper support ([dev.to, Blixild](https://dev.to/jacek_garbuliski/inkling-2-mini-vs-qwen38-27b-and-arete-27b-on-agent-tasks-54ii); [vLLM PR #69028](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/69028)).

The cheaper path is to tune Qwen first. AA's data shows that moving Qwen from `xhigh` to `medium` cost only 2 index points (20 to 18) and barely moved AA-Briefcase (1391 to 1369). It also **cut output tokens by about 30% (128M to 89.3M) and time per task from 31.2 to 25.6 minutes** ([AA comparison](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/inkling-2-mini-vs-qwen3-8-27b); [AA Qwen page](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/qwen3-8-27b)). The same table shows Qwen's AA-Omniscience score falling from −6 to −36 at medium, so facts need checking on his own jobs. Alibaba also warns that lower effort can cause retries in multi-turn agent loops ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B)). His measured 70 tokens/s is well below the 189 tokens/s one tester got from FP8 Qwen with its built-in draft head on an H200. If his server is not using that draft head, enabling it is another free speed lever. That is an inference, because his GPU model and server settings are not in the repo. So the recommendation is a sequence, not a switch. First, ask the GPU server for `medium` effort and engine-enforced JSON on a sample of real jobs, and compare quality, valid-JSON rate and wall-clock time against today's runs. Then, once Inkling's vLLM image is confirmed on his exact GPU, run the same jobs through Inkling 2 Mini. Switch only if it matches Qwen on his own writing rubric and the time saving survives on his hardware. Both models are Apache 2.0, so licensing is no obstacle either way ([HF Qwen LICENSE](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B/blob/main/LICENSE); [HF Inkling card](https://huggingface.co/thinkingmachines/Inkling-2-Mini)). The decision also has a clock on it. Alibaba says open-weight "smaller-scale models from the Qwen4 series will soon be released" but gives no names, sizes or dates ([Alibaba Group, 16 Nov 2026](https://www.alibabagroup.com/en-US/document-2060139246777270272)). A small Qwen4 could reset the comparison within weeks.

Several findings hold across labs, referees, YouTubers and users, and can go on screen with confidence. The two models tie on aggregate intelligence. Inkling is much cheaper and faster on hosted APIs. Both overthink at their defaults, and turning effort down is the universal fix. Inkling knows less and invents more, as its own lab admits. Inkling's first-week runtime problems were mostly plumbing that improved within days. Qwen leads human-preference votes on writing and multi-turn chat. And no one, on YouTube or elsewhere, has yet tested either model on long-form writing with structured JSON over long sessions. That last gap is the opening for the narrator's own test.

## Conclusion

The real finding is not which model is smarter. For a self-hoster, **shipped defaults and serving maturity now matter more than the architecture**. Both labs ship their models at token-hungry effort settings that flatter benchmarks and punish users ("the default configuration is essentially broken (all in the name of benchmaxxing)", as one Hacker News commenter put it ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50581260))). Most of Inkling's first-week reputation was set by runtimes rather than weights. Looping's genuine innovation shows up as memory and hosted-cost savings, not as fewer tokens or better prose. That makes "should I switch?" a premature question for the narrator. The better question, and a better video, is how much of his Qwen bill was the default all along. Then he can test Inkling on his own jobs once its tooling stops moving, before a small Qwen4 changes the comparison again.
