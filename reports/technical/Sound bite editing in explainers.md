# Cut the narrator, let real voices land

Narrated explainers make real sound bites feel authentic with one simple move. The narrator sets the clip up, **goes completely silent while the real person speaks**, and does not say the quoted words first. Broadcast textbooks, NPR's training material and the observed practice of CNBC, Bloomberg Originals and Johnny Harris all settle on the same default, the *attributed lead-in*: who is speaking, where, when and why it matters, but not the words themselves. Repetition is kept for after the clip. Visio Studio's planned "replace" mode is therefore not a gimmick. It automates the textbook default, as long as the narrator's lead-in still reads as a complete set-up once the quote is lifted out. The gap between impactful and gimmicky is mostly craft and restraint. That means using 3–15 s bites for emotion, personality or proof rather than facts. It also means matching the clip's level to the narrator, letting room tone hide the seam, ducking the music further (or dropping it) for the line, showing the speaker's face with a lower third that gives name, venue and year, and captioning the clip word for word. The hard limits are editorial: one continuous, unaltered span; a scripted quote that matches the clip word for word; honest dating of archive footage; and no placement that implies something the speaker never said. That last failure cost the BBC its director-general in 2025. Rights risk is real but manageable. Short commentary clips have a reasonable US fair-use argument, but Content ID can claim even a few seconds, and YouTube's reused-content review judges the whole channel. No mainstream AI video generator inserts verbatim sound bites automatically, so Visio Studio is building ahead of prior art. It should automate the mechanical checks and leave meaning, fairness and rights to the owner, most of all in the Huang resignation section. Near narration about allegations, only his actual statements on the matter belong.

## Narrators stop for the tape and never say the line first

Broadcast TV's basic unit is the VO-SOT: "the anchor reads a voice-over portion, then **stops talking** while a sound bite is heard full" ([Writing for Electronic Media](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/writingforelectronicmedia/chapter/vosots/)). The same textbook advises choosing the bite first and then writing narration that leads "right into it", and school package guides say the same: type the source's exact words into the script before writing narration, "[to] avoid writing narration that repeats it" ([Bellville AV](https://bellvilleav.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Section-4-Write-the-Video-Package-Script.pdf)). NPR Training publishes the most complete toolbox for getting into and out of tape. Ways in: tease and back-ID, the hint, question then answer, the signpost, a general set-up followed by the specific bite, and starting a thought that the tape completes. Ways out: react, echo a word from the tape, take the next step, emphasise or repeat, and complete the speaker's sentence. NPR calls the default "Person X, who has XX job, says Y" form "OK, and sometimes it's the best choice, but... boring" when it is the only device used ([NPR Training toolbox](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-66787/writing-through-sound-a-toolbox-for-getting-into-and-out-of-your-tape)). Across every endorsed pattern, the narrator varies only what comes before and after the bite. **The narrator is never heard during it.** The one overlap pattern, "lip flap" (a person visibly talking while the reporter's voice-over plays), is "viewed as unflattering... and discouraged in industry circles" ([Bucy & Grabe 2007](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286745582_Image_Bite_Politics_News_and_the_Visual_Framing_of_Elections)).

### Paraphrase the idea, never the words

Every writing guide in the research gives the same answer to "should the narrator say the line first?": **no**. "You also don't want the bite to repeat what the anchor just said" ([UEN Pressbooks](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/writingforelectronicmedia/chapter/vosots/)). "Don't use the same phrasing in your intro that's used in the bite" ([CBI, Writing for the Ear](https://www.askcbi.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Writing-for-the-Ear-NSEMC-2019-FINAL-1.pdf)). Parroting "makes the audience feel like they're hearing double" ([broadcast-script tutorial](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2xGvoem04Do)). NPR's Alex Chadwick suggests what to say instead: rather than reporting that the mayor will run and then playing the mayor saying so, tell listeners whether the announcement was expected or a surprise, and whether he sounded confident or worried ([Transom](https://transom.org/2015/alex-chadwick/)). Repetition is endorsed in only two places: **after** the tape (NPR's echo and emphasis moves), and when tape is murky. One NPR editor is "a big believer in restating key phrases from murky actualities" ([NPR Storytelling](https://nprstorytelling.tumblr.com/post/118461686173/just-because-your-tape-is-hard-to-hear-doesnt)). The guides also ban filler lead-ins: "If you set up a bite with HAD THIS TO SAY, you are doing it wrong" ([UEN Pressbooks](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/writingforelectronicmedia/chapter/vosots/)).

Successful YouTubers break the rule deliberately, and the way they do it is instructive. In *The Problem With Elon Musk*, Johnny Harris says "At the age of 12, he invented a video game," and Musk then says nearly the same words. Harris continues "That he sold to a magazine for," and the clip lands "$500," a ping-pong with bites as short as ~0.8 s ([Johnny Harris](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WYQxG4KEzvo)). Elsewhere in the video he echoes one keyword ("bullied") and lets the clip add the vivid detail ("I got punched in the face many times"). ColdFusion's *Meteoric Rise of Nvidia* takes the riskier route: it paraphrases Huang's "nobody in their right mind would do it" at length, then plays a ~67 s clip that says it again ([ColdFusion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nVtb2vNUOdU)). Repetition seems to work when the bite is short and adds detail, emotion or proof that it is really him. It fails when the viewer already has the full content before a long clip. No audience-retention data exists to confirm either reading.

The patterns relevant to Visio Studio are below. "Replace-mode fit" asks whether the pattern is just *narrator stops → clip → narrator resumes*, which replace mode produces mechanically.

The table's main lesson is that **replace mode already supports five of NPR's patterns**, because they share one timeline and differ only in how the lead-in and write-out are worded. The same Huang–TSMC quote shows the natural fallback relationship: CNBC plays it as a clip, while ColdFusion has the narrator say "in his own words, quote" and read it ([CNBC](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d3L2uPuxOxU); [ColdFusion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nVtb2vNUOdU)). Replace mode upgrades a narrator-read quote to a real clip when one is approved.

### Short, sparse and saved for what only the speaker can say

Bite length has shrunk for decades. The average network-news sound bite fell from **43 s in 1968 to 9 s in 1988** ([Hallin 1992](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1992.tb00775.x)) and to **7.73 s by 2004** ([Bucy & Grabe](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286745582_Image_Bite_Politics_News_and_the_Visual_Framing_of_Elections)). Teaching norms put bites at **5–15 s**, "though the closer you get to 15, the more you must justify the added length" ([UEN Pressbooks](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/writingforelectronicmedia/chapter/vosots/)). NPR keeps tease tape under 10 s. In the team's YouTube sample, clip-led docs ran dense. CNBC's Nvidia film used about 11 bites in its first five minutes, roughly 4–28 s each ([CNBC](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d3L2uPuxOxU)). Harris ran ~3.7 bites a minute, mostly 2–5 s ([Harris](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WYQxG4KEzvo)). Narration-led docs like ColdFusion's used essentially one long real-voice clip as a climax. That gives two house styles. Visio Studio, with a continuous AI narrator and a few 3–15 s clips per video, belongs to the **narration-led style, but should borrow the clip-led channels' lead-in discipline**. These YouTube counts come from a small sample and are not a published study.

What makes a bite worth playing is equally consistent across sources. Apple's documentary guide: "the script should provide the facts and set up the sound bites, which provide the emotions and the stories" ([Apple Education](https://www.apple.com/education/docs/documentary_guide_12-11.pdf)). NPR's soundbite essentials list good bites as emotional, passionate, genuine, vivid and memorable. Bites to avoid carry basic facts, numbers, lists or verbosity, or need "a lot of set up to make the soundbite clear" ([NPR Training, Soundbite essentials](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2026/01/26/g-s1-104552/sound-bites-choose-good-audio-clips)). A second NPR piece warns against treating tape as text, which matters for a transcript-driven product: "It's tempting to think of actualities as quotes. Especially nowadays with the availability of transcription software… This is a mistake. **What the words say may be less important than how they are said**" ([NPR Training, radio reporting tips](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2026/02/05/g-s1-107659/radio-reporting-tips)). Harris says he auditioned "probably 10 different sound bites" for his opening and chose one for its tone ([The Rise of Johnny Harris](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fa1_VWuIElE)). Huang's "I wish upon you ample doses of pain and suffering," from his March 2024 Stanford SIEPR Economic Summit appearance ([Startup Archive](https://www.startuparchive.org/p/jensen-huang-greatness-comes-from-character)), passes every test: it is short, surprising, first-person, and something only he could say.

## Matched loudness, room tone and a deeper music duck hide the seam

Published standards cover only loudness and captions. Split-edit lengths, duck depths and lower-third timing are practitioner conventions, many from 2026 vendor blogs that may copy each other. The numbers below are good starting values, not standards, and the recommendations table later marks which is which.

### Sound: no overlapping voices, no loudness jump

Practitioners give **6–14 frames (0.25–0.6 s)** for softening dialogue cuts and 12–36 frames (0.5–1.5 s) for cutting into or out of an interview over B-roll. Two hard rules: never split "when a mouth is still moving", and keep cuts straight on punchlines ([nolanlabs](https://nolanlabs.io/blog/j-cut-and-l-cut)). Split edits should go on roughly 30–50% of dialogue cuts, not all of them ([VideoCue](https://videocue.io/guides/the-j-cut-and-the-l-cut)). For a narrator-plus-clip timeline, any overlap should be picture over silence or room tone, **never narrator speech over clip speech**. Room tone is what hides the join: "When room tone suddenly goes missing, the brain notices. It becomes clear that the performance was artificially edited" ([iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/community/blog/basics-of-room-tone-audio-editing)). A reality-TV mixer layers ambience under joined bites for the same reason ([postPerspective](https://postperspective.com/emails/iZotope/ppnews-iZotope-161004.html)). Replace mode puts a clip with its own background next to clean synthetic narration, so the transition is a change of source, not a gap to fill. Extending the clip's own ambience a quarter to half a second past its edges, with short fades, is the practical answer.

On loudness, EBU R 128 sets programme loudness at **−23 LUFS with true peak ≤ −1 dBTP** ([EBU R 128](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128v5_0.pdf)). Netflix gates on dialogue at −27 LKFS ±2 LU, because "dialogue is the anchor element" ([Netflix](https://studiopartner.netflix.net/studio/branded-sound-mix-spec-and-best-practices); [Production Expert](https://www.production-expert.com/production-expert-1/how-to-optimise-an-audio-mix-for-delivery-to-netflix)). YouTube publishes no target. The commonly cited **−14 LUFS is an observation** from "Stats for nerds", and YouTube turns loud audio down but never turns quiet audio up ([lufsready](https://lufsready.com/blog/loudness-normalization-explained)). Big channels vary: one third-party spot check measured Wendover at −24.3 LUFS and found a 50-video median of −16.3 ([Umbrella Creators](https://umbrellacreators.com/blog/how-to-normalize-audio-for-youtube)). What matters within one video is that bite and narrator speech sit at the same level. A professional doc mixer uses the studio interview "as my baseline" and works through de-noise, de-reverb, de-crackle, EQ match and then levels. He keeps "as much robustness and low-end as possible" rather than polishing archive audio to sound like the studio ([postPerspective](https://postperspective.com/emails/iZotope/ppnews-iZotope-161004.html)). Viewers accept that a real clip sounds different from the narrator. They do not accept a jump in volume.

Music needs a deeper duck under a bite than under narration. W3C's accessibility guidance puts background sound **at least 20 dB below foreground speech** ([W3C WAI](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/av-content/)). Practitioners report 12–18 dB ducks for general YouTube and 15–20 dB for dense explainer narration ([tryuncle](https://tryuncle.com/learn/davinci-resolve/how-to-duck-background-music-under-dialogue-in-davinci-resolve)), with manual moves that start "200ms early, over half a second" to avoid pumping ([addaly](https://addaly.com/learn/motion-and-audio/ducking-and-beds)). Clip audio is noisier and lower in fidelity than a narrator, so masking hurts it more, which argues for an extra few dB under bites. For the key line, a recognised technique is to **pull the music out entirely**: "Right before the powerful soundbite lands, the music cuts out… The soundbite plays clean." The same agency limits this to two or three moments per video so it keeps its weight ([Full Send](https://fullsendagency.co/blog/how-music-editing-in-a-brand-video-actually-works)). Dropping to pure room tone "signals to the viewer that something different is happening" ([Editing Luke](https://www.editingluke.net/breathing-room-editors-embrace-silence/)). Risers and whooshes before a bite belong to high-energy brand edits and would push a documentary-style explainer toward gimmick.

### Picture and text: face first, name it, date it, caption it verbatim

A broadcast script example puts the speaker "on camera for first 4 seconds of SOT; add lower third… Cover remaining jump cut with matching shop B-roll" ([Bellville AV](https://bellvilleav.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Section-4-Write-the-Video-Package-Script.pdf)). Seeing the face first is how viewers know the voice is real, and in an AI-narrated video that proof is the whole point of the clip. No authoritative standard exists for framed treatments (rounded cards, TV or phone frames). They are house style, best kept for low-resolution sources, burned-in tickers or aspect mismatches. For 9:16, a full-height crop keeps only **31.6%** of a 16:9 frame ([make-vertical](https://make-vertical.com/en/16-9-to-9-16-converter)). A square window on a blurred background keeps 56% of the width, and plain black letterboxing "looks like a repost" ([trezalabs](https://www.trezalabs.com/blog/how-to-convert-horizontal-video-to-vertical)). Fake "archival" grain on recent footage misleads about the date, and BBC guidance says archive "should not be used in a way that materially misleads" ([BBC Editorial Guidelines, Accuracy](https://www.bbc.co.uk/editorialguidelines/documents/section-3-accuracy.pdf)).

Lower thirds go up "on the person's first substantial line, not their first appearance… Two to three seconds after they start speaking," once per person unless the piece is long. They should never cover the end of a sentence that matters, because "the eye goes to new text on screen and stops listening for about a second" ([Addaly](https://addaly.com/learn/video-editing/name-supers-and-credits)). Hold times are given as 4–6 s ([Cut.express](https://cut.express/blog/lower-thirds)), with "any longer than seven seconds" too much ([Hencar](https://hencar.com/2015/12/what-the-font-when-to-use-onscreen-text/)). Dating is an honesty requirement, not decoration. The BBC requires making it clear "when content is not contemporaneous" ([BBC Section 13](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/use-of-bbc-content-after-publication-or-broadcast/guidelines)). NPR says listeners "should not be left to think that any archival or previously obtained audio was gathered in the context of the current story" ([NPR Ethics Handbook](https://current.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/01/NPR-Ethics-Handbook-5.2.2012-Final-Edition.pdf)). A two-line super, with the name on the first line and "role at the time · venue, month year" on the second, covers identification and dating in one element.

Captions must "identify who is speaking" ([WCAG 1.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/captions-prerecorded)). The BBC prefers colour for this, keeps each speaker's colour fixed, conventionally shows the narrator in yellow, and notes that double quotes can mark "a quotation from a person" ([BBC Subtitle Guidelines](https://www.bbc.co.uk/accessibility/forproducts/guides/subtitles/)). Netflix uses italics for off-screen narrators and for media playback when the speaker isn't in the scene, never for a visible speaker. It also asks that documentary clips be transcribed in full ([Netflix Timed Text Style Guide](https://studiopartner.netflix.net/studio/us-english-timed-text-style-guide)). DCMP adds a rule that bites an automated pipeline easily: do not name a speaker in captions "until the speaker is introduced in the audio or by an onscreen text/graphic" ([DCMP](https://dcmp.org/learn/603-captioning-key---speaker-identification)). The clip's captions should come from the clip's own verified transcript, never from the script's version of the quote.

### Trimming: whole thoughts, small handles, no internal edits

Broadcast scripts log each bite's duration and out-cue, its last three or four words ([news-script tutorial](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2xGvoem04Do)), and guides ask for "a little extra picture and sound before and after the chosen words" ([Bellville AV](https://bellvilleav.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Section-4-Write-the-Video-Package-Script.pdf)). Practitioners note that a breath before a phrase lasts 200–600 ms and is a better cut point than the frame where a mouth opens ([kookenhoomen](https://kookenhoomen.com/motion/cut-the-sequence-on-the-sound/)). Clean-ups are allowed within limits. Removing ums and stumbles is "most of the time… fine, as long as you are not removing something that suggested the interviewee was reluctant or unable to respond" ([NPR Training](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-65686/how-to-decide-what-to-cut-or-not-in-an-interview)). For presidents and major candidates, however, NPR does no internal edits at all, because making someone "sound too coherent… may be changing the tone" ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/11/14/nx-s1-5609180/how-npr-edits-remarks-by-the-president)). Real speech is messier than a scripted quote. In the real audio, Huang's line comes after a false start: "...and so if I could wish upon you I don't know how to do it for all of you Stanford students, I wish upon you ample doses of pain and suffering" ([YouTube excerpt](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vOvQSqY7Jgc)). Cut points must be chosen on the audio, not the script. Starting at the clean restart ("I wish upon you ample doses…") is a single contiguous span and needs no internal edit.

## One continuous span, the exact words and an honest date

### Journalism codes allow shortening but forbid any change of meaning

AP: "Internal editing of audio soundbites of newsmakers is not permitted. Shortened soundbites by cutaway or other video transition are permitted as long as the speaker's meaning is not altered." AP also does "not alter quotations, even to correct grammatical errors" ([AP via Accountable Journalism](https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/news-values-and-principles)). PBS says jumps within remarks need "an audio or visual cue (such as a flash, wipe, or different camera)." It specifically says "seamlessly editing together two separate statements while showing B-roll would generally not be sufficiently transparent." It also rejects composite sentences outright, and cites *Masson v. New York Magazine*, under which both a fabricated quotation and "an exact quotation out of context" can support a US defamation claim ([PBS Standards](https://www.pbs.org/standards/blogs/standards-memos/handling-quotations/)). The BBC forbids inter-cutting that creates "a materially misleading impression of events or a contribution." It requires AI use to be "transparent and clear to the audience," and treats any AI product that curates content as "subject to editorial approval and human oversight" ([BBC Editorial Guidelines](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)). NPR requires an actuality to "reflect accurately what that person was asked, was responding to or was addressing" ([NPR Accuracy](https://www.npr.org/about-npr/688139552/accuracy)). Ofcom treats re-using material recorded for another purpose as fair in itself, which describes every explainer built from archive clips, but requires contributions to be "represented fairly" ([Ofcom Section 7](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/tv-radio-and-on-demand/broadcast-codes/broadcast-code/section-seven-fairness)). These codes don't bind a YouTube creator. They are still the best yardstick for what is defensible, and they track the same harms that defamation law and platforms care about.

Two cases show how short clips go wrong. In 2010, a ~2.5-minute excerpt of Shirley Sherrod's speech, taken from roughly 43–45 minutes, was posted as evidence of racism and she was forced to resign. The full tape showed "the climax, not to mention the moral, of Sherrod's tale had been edited out" ([FactCheck.org](https://www.factcheck.org/2010/07/shirley-sherrods-contextual-nightmare/)). In 2024, BBC Panorama joined two Trump remarks made **54 minutes apart** into "We fight like hell," with no wipe or caption, and then cut to Proud Boys footage. An internal memo said this "materially misled viewers" ([Telegraph, memo](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/2025/11/06/read-devastating-internal-bbc-memo-in-full/)). Director-general Tim Davie and News CEO Deborah Turness resigned on 9 November 2025 ([BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c3vn25d5dq7o)). From these cases the research derives six tests:

- Turn: does the speaker reverse or qualify the point just after the out-point?
- Voice: is the speaker giving their own view, or voicing someone else's?
- Splice: is the clip one continuous span?
- Adjacency: do the narration, B-roll or music around the clip imply something false?
- Full footage: would a viewer who later watched the whole source feel deceived?
- Recognition: would the speaker recognise their own meaning?

The first three can be partly automated. The last three need a human.

### Quote fidelity and provenance must be checked by machine, then by ear

Quotation marks, or "he said" followed by a clip, promise exact words. AP's rule is that a flawed quote is paraphrased faithfully or dropped, never edited ([AP](https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/news-values-and-principles)). NPR's rule is that verbatim audio must be signalled as the source's own wording ([NPR Ethics: Honesty](https://www.npr.org/about-npr/688367308/honesty)). The research proposes four fidelity classes, which are a product design rather than an industry standard:

- **Exact**: the words match after normalising filler words.
- **Near**: contractions or a dropped filler differ; rewrite the script to the clip's words.
- **Paraphrase**: the meaning matches but the words don't; it must not be presented as a quote.
- **Mismatch**: block the clip.

The Huang line shows why this matters. At SIEPR he says "ample doses of pain and suffering," but when Patrick Collison quoted it back to him at Stripe Sessions, he restated it as "plenty of pain and suffering" ([Stripe conversation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8Pfa8kPjUio)). Two real clips carry two different wordings, so the script's quote has to follow whichever clip is approved. Collison also placed the event "at the GSB, I think." SIEPR is the more specific attribution and the one to use in a lead-in or lower third.

Provenance is the other half. Verification practice starts "from the assumption that a video has been 'scraped' or duplicated," and holds that "one piece of evidence alone is insufficient" ([Verification Handbook](https://datajournalism.com/read/handbook/verification-1/verifying-video/5-verifying-video)). "A perfect deepfake detection system will never exist," and people "could not reliably detect" modified lip movements ([Verification Handbook 3](https://files.ejc.net/production/resources/2020/verification-handbook-3-for-disinformation-and-media-manipulation/Verification-Handbook-3.pdf)). For keynotes this points to preferring the official first-party upload. It also means rejecting auto-dubbed tracks, interpreters, a host *reading* the quote, and "AI" or parody uploads, and treating diarisation, a face-on-screen check and voice matching as signals that add up, not as verdicts. AP adds that translations must be faithful and labelled ([AP](https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/news-values-and-principles)).

### The Huang section needs separated tiers and no implied replies

The facts as reported: Huang resigned as Nvidia CEO, effective immediately, on 12 November 2026, after reports of extramarital affairs. The *New York Times* reported one with "a businesswoman U.S. intelligence suspects to be a Chinese spy, which Beijing denies" ([AP](https://apnews.com/article/nvidia-jensen-huang-resigns-ceo-affairs-314714d463cf578cbb0dbd49de903107)). The board's statement did not mention the spying allegations, and Huang had earlier admitted infidelity ([Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/12/nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-resigns)). The Attorney General said the Justice Department "has not yet reached a conclusion" ([Reuters via Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-resigns-in-blow-to-ai-boom-5021980)). Huang's statement reads: "I did not live up to the standards expected of everyone at NVIDIA… I own my mistakes completely." NPR "has not independently confirmed those reports," and closed-door House sessions are set for **1–2 December** ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/17/nx-s1-6044213/jensen-huang-nvidia-congress)). The board's independent members plan to keep investigating ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/nvidia-investigation-jensen-huang-continue-departure-sources-say-rcna609136)).

The standards require separating three tiers. Established facts are the resignation, his admission and the board statements. Reported allegations should be attributed to the outlets that reported them, with "not independently confirmed" carried where those outlets use it. Denials include Beijing's. His own statement should be included as his reply ([Ofcom 7.11](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/tv-radio-and-on-demand/broadcast-codes/broadcast-code/section-seven-fairness); [BBC 3.4.22](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)). The concrete danger for this project is adjacency. Huang has a widely reused bite, "Every company makes mistakes and I make a lot of them," which CNBC ran in 2023 and reused with an almost identical lead-in in its 2026 *Nvidia In Crisis* piece ([CNBC 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d3L2uPuxOxU); [CNBC 2026](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Yx2lgDruVaI)). Placed after narration about the allegations, a line about GPU bets would read as a confession. That is the Panorama B-roll problem in miniature. Because the story is still moving, content prepared in advance "must be checked to make sure it has not been overtaken by events" ([BBC 3.4.33](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)).

### Rights: a fair-use argument, Content ID friction and channel-level policy

None of this is legal advice. In the US, short commentary clips from factual keynotes and news have a reasonable fair-use argument, but courts decide case by case. Credit alone does not make a use fair ("There aren't any magic words"), and Content ID can block monetisation "even if you only use a few seconds"; the remedy is a dispute ([YouTube Help: Fair use](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9783148?hl=en)). The documentary fair-use code asks that quotations be attributed, drawn from a range of sources, and "no longer than is necessary," and that they not be used just to avoid shooting equivalent footage ([CMSI](https://cmsimpact.org/code/documentary-filmmakers-statement-of-best-practices-in-fair-use/)). The UK and India are narrower. The UK requires a listed purpose (criticism, review, quotation, or reporting current events) plus acknowledgement ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/exceptions-to-copyright)). India has "no 'safe harbour' for duration" ([Bar & Bench](https://www.barandbench.com/columns/youtubers-in-peril-why-indias-fair-use-laws-need-urgent-clarification)), and a Delhi court held that "a few seconds, can be the heart of it" ([Legal Republic](https://www.legalrepublic.in/everyday-law/youtube-pirated-copyright-takedown/)).

Licensing every bite is not realistic. Agencies such as Reuters license for editorial use only ([Reuters Connect](https://www.reutersconnect.com/transactional-license-terms)), and market estimates put news archive at **$80–100 per second with 30 s minimums** ([DFI Rentals](https://www.dfirentals.com/news/archival-footage-licensing-cost)). C-SPAN allows non-commercial posting of federal-event coverage with attribution. Monetised use needs a licence, and its terms restrict use in AI systems ([C-SPAN](https://www.c-span.org/about/copyrightsAndLicensing/); [C-SPAN Terms](https://www.c-span.org/about/termsAndConditions/)). Nvidia's newsroom hosts keynote footage, but no usage licence was found ([NVIDIA Newsroom](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/multimedia/corporate/corporate-b-roll)).

Two YouTube policies matter separately from copyright. First, reused and "inauthentic" content is judged **at the channel level** and "is not based on copyright, permission, or fair use." It allows clips "where you add a storyline and commentary" but excludes "AI-generated content made with generic or unoriginal templates" ([YouTube monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)). Second, AI disclosure is required when content "makes a real person appear to say or do something they didn't do." An AI narrator speaking over unaltered clips is not on the list, but a photorealistic AI presenter arguably counts as a "realistic scene," and disclosure "alone won't limit a video's audience" ([YouTube GenAI disclosure](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)).

## Existing tools index by transcript but distrust their own word edges

Mainstream editors treat the transcript as the editing surface and then heal the cuts. Descript's "Avoid harsh cuts" **skips** any filler-word removal "that can't be removed without clipping into nearby words or leaving behind an awkward cut" ([Descript Help](https://help.descript.com/script-editing/filler-words)). It builds room tone from non-speech audio in the first 30 seconds of a file ([Descript room tone](https://help.descript.com/effects-animations-transitions/room-tone)), and offers "Smooth" regeneration and "Restore removed media" at edit boundaries ([Descript edit boundaries](https://help.descript.com/script-editing/edit-boundaries)). In Premiere, deleting transcript text applies a ripple edit ([Adobe](https://helpx.adobe.com/premiere/desktop/edit-projects/edit-video-using-text-based-editing/detect-and-delete-pauses-in-transcripts.html)). DaVinci Resolve's IntelliScript is the closest analogue to Visio's design: it matches script lines against transcribed clips, puts the best match on the main track and **alternates on disabled tracks**. Blackmagic still warns that heavy rewriting of transcripts "will have unpredictable effects" ([Resolve 21 manual](https://docs.imaster.org/davinci-resolve-21-manual/part-6-edit/ch.-47-text-based-editing)). None of these vendors publishes default fade or padding values, and all of them expect a human to review the cuts.

For finding clips, Filmot searches YouTube captions with exact-phrase and proximity operators and an API. It returns caption-line start times, not word boundaries ([Filmot FAQ](https://filmot.com/faq/1321/What%20endpoints%20does%20the%20Filmot%20API%20offer%2C%20and%20what%20does%20subtitle%20search%20return)), and its index "may predate the video's current captions" ([filmot-cli](https://github.com/baba20o/filmot-cli/blob/main/AGENTS_README.md)). Opus Clip and similar tools rank moments by "virality potential," not by verbatim match ([Opus Help](https://help.opus.pro/docs/article/9947095-clip-anything)). The research found **no mainstream AI generator** (InVideo, Pictory, Fliki, HeyGen, Synthesia) that inserts real people's sound bites. The precedents are 2026 agent projects. VideoDB's open-source pipeline gathers YouTube clips and checks "scene by scene, that what the narration says matches what is on screen" ([VideoDB](https://videodb.io/blog/agentic-streams.md)). A self-reported newscast, Neon Bulletin, cuts "a verbatim 5 to 20 second quote" with Whisper word timestamps, "capped at four per show with speaker, date, and source on screen." It normalises voices to −16 LUFS and keeps uploads private until reviewed ([Neon Bulletin](https://expertcoders.net/portfolio/view/neon-bulletin)). Both converge on caps, on-screen attribution and human approval.

The technical findings bear directly on replace mode. yt-dlp section downloads fall back to the previous keyframe, which can be **9–10 s early**, unless you re-encode with `--force-keyframes-at-cuts` ([yt-dlp issue #8011](https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/issues/8011); [yt-dlp FAQ](https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/wiki/FAQ)). The better approach is to download a padded window and cut locally. YouTube auto-captions carry only word *start* times, in an undocumented format ([Corpora](https://globalcorpora.com/blog/yt-dlp-download-subtitles-auto-captions/)). On 2026 benchmarks, Montreal Forced Aligner places word boundaries within **~20 ms**, while ASR-bundled timestamps are coarser ([arXiv 2606.18466](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18466)). **Parakeet-TDT reports on an 80 ms grid with roughly 80 ms mean error**. That figure was read from a table whose headers were lost in extraction, so treat it as approximate. The same benchmark found large systematic biases (Whisper runs ~150 ms early), found utterance *end* boundaries the hardest to place, and found that re-aligning ASR output improved boundary accuracy by 40% ([FA-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.32396)). Diarisation remains imperfect. pyannote Community-1 scores 8.9% DER on broadcast TV but 26.7% on telephone conversation ([pyannote model card](https://huggingface.co/pyannote/speaker-diarization-community-1)), so it can raise a flag but shouldn't make decisions on its own.

## Recommendations for Visio Studio's replace-mode sound bites

### Ship one timeline and five scripts first; defer clip-first patterns

Support the **attributed lead-in** first, because it is exactly what replace mode produces, and it is the broadcast default. Set-up-then-confirm, question-then-answer, clip-then-react/echo and fact-then-evidence come almost for free. They use the same *narrator stops → clip → narrator resumes* timeline and differ only in script wording, so treat them as lead-in and write-out styles that the script generator can choose between, not as separate features. The fallback, used when no clip is approved, is the **narrator-read quote**. It should have attribution at the start ("In his own words:" or "As Huang put it:") and an on-screen quote card in quotation marks. A lead-in written this way works for both outcomes, which the research identifies as the key to making replace mode safe. Defer the sentence-completion ping-pong, because the clip must start mid-thought and that conflicts with the whole-thought trimming rule. Defer tease and back-ID, cold opens, montages and butt cuts too, because they need a separate "insert" mode where the clip has no narrator text to replace. Never support lip flap. Expect a timing change: the clip's length will rarely equal the narrator's reading, so captions, B-roll, graphics and music cues after the quote must reflow. That is an engineering consequence the research didn't address, not a sourced finding.

### Script rules the generator and a linter can enforce

| Rule | Why | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The quote must be a whole sentence standing on its own. Partial quotes embedded in narration ("he wished them 'ample doses' of…") are never replaceable and stay narrator-read | The clip must start and end on a whole thought (PBS; whole-sentence trimming) | Replaceable: `He put it to the students as a wish: "I wish upon you ample doses of pain and suffering."` |
| The lead-in must read as a complete set-up with the quote removed: no dangling "that", no "said," left mid-sentence, attribution at the start | Replace mode deletes the quote; the fallback needs start attribution | Bad: `Huang said that "…"` · Good: `At a Stanford economics summit in 2024, Huang offered an unusual wish:` |
| The lead-in shares no run of three or more content words with the quote, and never paraphrases its punchline | The no-parroting rule (UEN, CBI, NPR) | Bad: `He wished them pain and suffering:` then the clip |
| No filler hand-offs ("had this to say", "tells us more", "said the following") | UEN, Bellville | — |
| The lead-in supplies the context needed to keep the meaning (the question, the argument, the occasion) | Whole-meaning rule; NPR "what that person was asked" | `Huang argues greatness comes from character built through setbacks.` before the wish |
| The venue and date in the lead-in match the clip's recording metadata | Archive must be dated honestly (BBC 13.4.4; NPR) | "Stanford SIEPR summit, March 2024", not "Stanford graduates" or "GSB" |
| Quote only emotion, opinion, personality or proof; narration carries facts and numbers | Apple, NPR soundbite essentials | Don't quote "Revenue grew 200%" |
| The write-out adds new information or reacts; it may echo one word after the clip, never restate the clip | NPR out-of-tape techniques | `Pain and suffering. He meant it, and Nvidia's early years supplied plenty.` |
| On approval, overwrite the script's quote with the clip's verbatim transcript | The fallback and the captions then match what was actually said (AP) | "ample doses" (SIEPR) vs "plenty of" (Stripe) |
| In sensitive sections, clips must be about the matter itself, or be clearly labelled neutral identification footage | Adjacency and juxtaposition rules (BBC 3.4.25) | No "mistakes" bite after allegation narration |

### Default editing values

These are starting values. "Sourced" means a standard or authoritative guide. "Practitioner" means low-authority craft consensus. "Inference" means the research team's own reasoning. Tune them by ear on the first project.

| Setting | Default | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Clip length | 3–15 s hard bounds; aim for 4–12 s; warn above 12 s | Sourced (5–15 s textbook norm) plus inference |
| Density | Start at about 4 bites per 10 minutes, with at least 20–30 s of narration between bites | Inference; the newscast precedent caps at 4 per show |
| Clip in-point | First word onset − 80–150 ms, snapped to the nearest low-energy gap, never inside another speaker's word | Inference; Parakeet's ~80 ms grid |
| Clip out-point | Last word offset + 150–300 ms inside the following breath; extend to the silence midpoint if the gap is under 400 ms; more slack on the tail than the head | Inference; end boundaries are hardest (FA-Bench) |
| Edge refinement | Optionally re-align only the two edge words with a forced aligner, and calibrate Parakeet's average signed offset on a few hand-labelled clips | Inference from the MFA and FA-Bench findings |
| Unclean edge | No clean gap at an edge, or another voice inside the window: reject the candidate and keep the narrator fallback | Descript's "skip if it can't be cut cleanly" precedent |
| Narrator cut | End the narrator at the lead-in's last-word offset plus its natural trailing breath, snapped to a low-energy point; resume at the next phrase onset | Inference (unsourced; listen to the prosody) |
| Hand-off to clip | Clip picture appears 0.2–0.4 s before the first word, filled with the clip's own room tone; voices never overlap | Practitioner plus inference |
| Return | Narrator resumes 250–500 ms after the last word; hold the speaker's face through the beat; clip room tone tails 6–12 frames | Practitioner plus inference |
| Audio edges | 10–30 ms fades on speech edges; 100–250 ms on ambience; extend clip room tone 0.25–0.5 s past both edges | Inference; iZotope room-tone rationale |
| Clip clean-up | High-pass at 80–100 Hz, gentle de-noise, optional low-pass at 8–12 kHz for hissy archive, light compression; never change speed or pitch; don't over-clean | Inference from a doc mixer's workflow |
| Bite loudness | Match the bite's speech-gated loudness to the narrator's within ±1 LU, using static gain (no dynamic normalisation on a 5 s clip) | Inference by analogy with the EBU tolerance |
| Final mix | −14 LUFS integrated, −1 dBTP true peak | Observed YouTube reference; EBU true-peak limit |
| Music under narration | Bed 15–20 dB below speech | Practitioner |
| Music under a bite | At least 20 dB below the bite's speech (about 3–6 dB deeper than under narration); keyframed ramp starting 200 ms before the bite, release over 300–600 ms after | Sourced (W3C) plus practitioner |
| Key-bite music drop | Opt-in per bite: fade out over 300–500 ms, ending 200–400 ms before the first word; return 0–300 ms after; at most 2–3 per 10 minutes; only on clean clip audio | Practitioner plus inference |
| Risers and stings | Off by default | Inference |
| Landscape picture | Full-frame face for the whole continuous bite; framed card only for sources at or below 480p, sources with tickers, or aspect mismatches; punch-in no more than 1.3× on 1080p | Sourced (face first) plus inference |
| Vertical (9:16) | Smoothed face-tracked crop; square-on-blur for two-shots or slides; never black bars; captions in the blurred band | Practitioner |
| Archival styling | None unless the footage is genuinely old, and always with a year label | Sourced (BBC 3.4.32) |
| Lower third | Appears 0.3–0.5 s after the clip starts, holds 4–6 s, leaves before the punchline, 200–300 ms animations; first appearance only (repeat after a long gap). Line 1: "Jensen Huang". Line 2: "Nvidia CEO · Stanford SIEPR summit, Mar 2024" | Practitioner plus sourced dating rule |
| Bites under 4 s | Name in the lead-in plus a compact name tag | Inference |
| Source credit | Small corner credit for the whole bite, plus a description credit with a timestamped link | Sourced (BBC, NPR, CMSI attribution) plus inference |
| Bite captions | Verbatim from the approved clip transcript; one fixed colour per real speaker, distinct from the narrator; roman (not italic); narrator captions end at the cut; bite captions start on the first word; frame out any burned-in source captions | Sourced (WCAG, BBC, Netflix, DCMP, FCC) |
| Fallback read quote | Start attribution, an on-screen quote card in quotation marks, and narrator captions styled as a quotation | Practitioner (quote cards) plus inference |
| Internal edits | Off. If ever enabled: visible transition plus owner approval, never a seamless join under B-roll, never reordered | Sourced (AP, PBS, NPR) |

### Automate the mechanical checks; keep meaning, fairness and rights human

The checks below come from the accuracy research's checklist. Thresholds such as context-window length and juxtaposition distance are product choices; no standard sets them.

| Automated check | Action |
|---|---|
| Quote fidelity (script quote vs a second transcription of the clip) | Block a mismatch or paraphrase; for a near match, propose rewriting the script to the clip's words |
| Single continuous span from one source | Block any join |
| Original audio track only; dubbing, interpreter or host-reading detected | Block dubbed audio; flag the rest |
| Recording date established (separate from the upload date) | Block if missing |
| Real speech unaltered (no speed, pitch, word edits, cloning or lip-sync) | Hard rule in the renderer |
| Duration 3–15 s; clean gaps at both edges | Reject, fall back to the narrator |
| Whole-sentence boundaries at in and out | Flag |
| Turn and qualifier scan, 2–3 sentences either side ("but", "however", "I was wrong") | Flag, showing the context |
| Reported speech, hypotheticals, sarcasm or laughter near the clip | Flag |
| Question captured for interview answers | Store with the clip; flag if the script implies a different question |
| Low-confidence words in the transcript | Flag; captions use "[inaudible]" rather than guessing |
| Diarisation shows another voice; no face on screen; voice doesn't match a verified reference | Flag |
| Provenance: non-official channel, re-upload, an earlier upload exists | Flag |
| Synthetic or parody signals (YouTube labels, C2PA, "AI", "deepfake", "parody" in metadata) | Flag |
| Parroting (shared run of three or more content words) and banned hand-off phrases in the lead-in | Block on save, with a rewrite prompt |
| Clip recorded before an event narrated just before it | Flag; require an archive label |
| Clip within ~20 s of allegation, resignation or investigation sentences | Mandatory human review; block admission-like wording ("mistake", "sorry", "trust", "responsibility") unless the clip is about the matter |
| Role shown is the person's role at the recording date | Auto-label; flag conflicts |
| Seconds used per source video and channel | Flag heavy reliance on one source |
| Licence field (CC BY, US government, standard) | Auto-attribute CC BY; send standard-licence clips to the rights rationale |
| Loudness after gain within ±1 LU of the narrator | Auto-correct and log |
| Freshness re-check of named people and topics before publishing | Flag news since script lock; add an "information as of" card |
| AI narrator or presenter used | Add a description note; set YouTube's AI-use flag for the photorealistic presenter |

The owner signs off on each clip and on the whole video. For each clip, the owner should:

- watch it with 30–60 s of context either side and confirm that the speaker would recognise their own meaning;
- confirm the identity of the speaker and that the source is genuine;
- check the fallback quote word for word against the audio;
- record a one-line fair-use rationale for standard-licence clips (what point the clip serves, why this length, and that it is not the source's "heart").

For the video as a whole, the owner watches the full assembled cut once for effects of juxtaposition, music and B-roll, confirms that the video carries real original analysis (the channel-level reused-content risk), and keeps a corrections log. Each clip should store an audit trail: source URL and ID, channel, licence, upload and recording dates, in and out times, the verbatim transcript plus a context window, the question, the fidelity class, the speaker checks, the flags and how each was resolved, the reviewer and time, the rights rationale, and the generated label text. BBC guidance asks that research records be kept "in a way that allows double-checking" ([BBC](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)), and a written rationale also helps in Content ID disputes.

For the Nvidia project specifically, the "pain and suffering" line is a strong first bite. Use the SIEPR clip with a lead-in that supplies his resilience argument and the venue and year. Start at the clean restart, and confirm in context what he says immediately after. In the resignation section, no keynote bites should sit near allegation narration. His written statement should be narrator-read with start attribution and a dated quote card (no video of it was identified in the research). Any 2024 or 2025 clip there needs a visible date and "Nvidia CEO" as his role at the time. Schedule a re-check after the 1–2 December House sessions, and get legal review before monetised publication.

## Conclusion

The research turns the feature's main worry on its head. Splicing a real voice into AI narration sounds like a gimmick, but replace mode lands on the most conservative pattern in broadcast journalism. The real hazards are not seams in the audio, which matched loudness, room tone and short fades handle well. They are **meaning and adjacency**: a lead-in that parrots or misdates the clip, a trim that cuts before a speaker's turn, or a harmless line placed where it reads as a confession. The textbooks say to choose the bite first and write to it. Visio inverts that by writing the script first, so the quote-in-script design is only safe if approval writes the clip's verbatim words back into the script and every lead-in is written to work whether the clip plays or the narrator reads the quote.

One implication goes beyond the sources. In a video whose narrator and presenter are synthetic, a dated, labelled and unaltered clip of the real person may carry more evidential weight than the same clip would in a human-narrated documentary. That makes clips most valuable at moments of authenticity and emotion, and makes any misuse more damaging. No research yet tests this, and no published tool does what Visio is building. The first project's review logs, which record which flags fired, which clips the owner rejected and why, will be the best evidence for tuning these defaults.
