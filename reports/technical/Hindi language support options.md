# Speak Hindi verbatim, reason in English

Visio Studio should add Hindi with a **hybrid design**. Every language-model stage keeps its English instructions and English internal fields (story analysis, shot plans, visual prompts, IDs). The models read the author's Devanagari story directly rather than a translation. Every spoken or displayed Hindi word is either copied from the story or written natively in Hindi, and is never translated to English and back. This keeps the owner's instinct, English reasoning, but drops the translation step, and the codebase already does half of it: scenes are cut from the story by code, and dialogue must stay "in the story's own language". That leaves code work that is mostly one-day fixes, plus a per-project language setting, Devanagari fonts and per-language voices. The real blockers are three models on the GPU server. **The self-hosted Breeze TTS 2 speaks only English and Chinese.** The Parakeet speech recogniser almost certainly does not know Hindi, and would quietly fail every Hindi take and trigger paid re-generations. **MiniMax H3 does not list Hindi among its 11 stable dialogue languages.** Translating in and out would gain at most a point or two of comprehension, while erasing honorifics and register and replacing the author's words with a machine back-translation. Native Hindi throughout would pay roughly three times the tokens on every internal field, with no measured gain. Before building anything, spend a few days on cheap tests: Hindi audio through the current Parakeet, two or three Hindi dialogue shots through H3, GLM-5.3 against Qwen3.8 on real Hindi stories, and a native listening test of two or three Hindi voice services. One decision is urgent even without Hindi: Breeze TTS 2's licence makes self-hosted output non-commercial, so a monetized English channel already needs a different voice source. Do Hindi story projects first, since they have no captions today, and leave explainers (captions, word timings, number graphics, evidence) for a second phase.

## Decision summary: confirm the hybrid, pick one voice service, test before building

The table gives each decision with its recommendation. The later sections explain the reasoning and the alternatives.

| Decision | Recommendation | Why | Settled by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overall approach | **Hybrid**: English reasoning and internal fields; models read the original Hindi; spoken and displayed text stays verbatim Hindi | Keeps the author's words and honorifics, matches how the code already works, and costs the fewest tokens | Decide now |
| Hindi text-to-speech (TTS) | Shortlist **ElevenLabs v3**, **MiniMax Speech 2.8** and **BreezeBlue's hosted multilingual Breeze**, then choose by native listening test. Prefer a service whose paid plan also covers English commercially | Self-hosted Breeze has no Hindi, and its output is non-commercial | Phase 0 tests |
| Transcription and alignment | Send Hindi, by project language, to a Hindi recogniser (self-hosted **IndicConformer**, MIT licence, or a hosted service with word timings), and keep English on Parakeet. Add a Hindi aligner only when explainers come | Parakeet v3 has no Hindi and cannot be told the language | Parakeet test; full build in Phases 2–3 |
| Script accepted | **Devanagari only at launch.** Later, accept Romanized Hindi through one transliteration pass that the user confirms | Models, voice services and aligners all work best on Devanagari | Decide now; revisit later |

## The hybrid wins because the pipeline already copies Hindi word for word

Visio Studio already treats the author's words as untouchable, and that one design choice settles most of the architecture question. Call it the **verbatim chain**. The model returns only the opening words of each scene, and code cuts the scene text out of the story and checks that the pieces join back to the original exactly ([scene_discovery_writer.py](../backend/app/services/scene_discovery_writer.py#L340)). Narration and dialogue in each event must be copied "without changing, shortening, rephrasing or translating", and the prompt already says the text "stays in the story's own language" ([event_extraction_writer.py](../backend/app/services/event_extraction_writer.py#L282)). Speech units must hold exactly the event's words. The H3 video prompt is refused unless each spoken line appears inside its `<d>` dialogue tag ([shot_prompt_writer.py](../backend/app/services/shot_prompt_writer.py#L1288)). Meanwhile, **about 20 writers tell the model to write in English, and every one of them writes internal or visual fields**: definitions, shot plans, continuity notes, image and video prompt bodies ([codebase text audit](../research_notes/Hindi%20language%20support%20options/codebase_text_speech_pipeline.md)). In other words, the hybrid is roughly the design the app already has, applied to a second language.

Taken literally, the owner's idea of "converting language only at input and output" is the **English pivot**: translate the story to English, run the pipeline, then translate the spoken and displayed text back. Its weakness is exactly the verbatim chain. Scene slices, events and speech units would all be cut from the English translation. The Hindi that gets spoken would be a machine back-translation produced at the speech-unit stage, so the author's own words would never be heard. It also needs two new paid pipeline stages with their own staleness rules, and any re-translation that comes out differently would change the English text and mark every downstream stage out of date ([codebase text audit](../research_notes/Hindi%20language%20support%20options/codebase_text_speech_pipeline.md)). The research says what this costs. When GPT-4o wrote in English and then translated into Hindi, **use of third-person honorifics fell from 86.6% to 56.1%** ([EMNLP 2025](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.emnlp-main.966)). That is the aap/tum/tu distinction that carries character voice. LLM literary translations are "considerably more literal and less diverse" than human ones, and automatic metrics picked out the human translation at most 20% of the time ([arXiv 2410.18697](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18697)). So the pipeline could not even check its own round-trip quality. The gain on the other side is small. For Hindi on Global-MMLU with Llama-3.3-70B, native input scored 84.6, full translation 86.2, and **English instructions with the original Hindi input scored 85.7** ([arXiv 2604.16937](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2604.16937)). The hybrid captures most of the pivot's benefit without translating anything.

**Native Hindi throughout** fails for different reasons. Every "write in English" instruction would flip, and the English-word guards that police video prompts (music words, talking words) would stop catching anything. Hindi prompt bodies would go to image and video models the repository treats as English-prompted, which probably means translating them back to English anyway, ending up at the hybrid with extra cost ([codebase text audit](../research_notes/Hindi%20language%20support%20options/codebase_text_speech_pipeline.md)). It also pays the **Devanagari token tax** on every field. GLM 5.2 needed **192 tokens for a Hindi passage against 63 for the same English** ([synthorai](https://synthorai.io/blog/cheapest-llm-by-language/)), and GLM-5's tokenizer has only 138 Devanagari entries ([Karki benchmark](https://siddhantskarki.com/case-studies/nepali-tokenizer)). Under the hybrid, only the story text and the Hindi output fields pay that tax. Research on reasoning models points the same way: forcing reasoning in the input language hurts reasoning tasks but helps cultural ones ([arXiv 2505.17407](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17407)). So the analysis stages should stay in English, and the audience-facing creative fields (Hindi titles, callouts, YouTube copy) should be written natively in Hindi with the Hindi story in view. That is the hybrid's split.

| | Native Hindi throughout | English pivot (translate in and out) | **Hybrid (recommended)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| What the models read | Hindi story, Hindi instructions | A machine-translated English story | The original Hindi story, with English instructions |
| Internal fields written in | Hindi | English | English |
| Where spoken Hindi comes from | Copied from the story | Back-translated from English | Copied from the story, unchanged |
| Author's exact words spoken? | Yes | **No** | Yes |
| New pipeline stages | None | Two (translate in, translate back), size L | None |
| Token cost compared with English today | About 3× on every field (GLM) | Two extra paid calls per story | About 3× only on the story and Hindi fields |
| Code size beyond the shared work | Large: flip about 20 writers and the English guards | Large: new stages, alignment back to Hindi | Small: mostly one-day fixes |
| Biggest risk | Hindi visual prompts and blind guards | Lost honorifics, register and voice | The GPU-server models (the same for all three) |

Existing products with Hindi in and Hindi out work the hybrid way. Pocket FM lets Hindi writers produce AI audio series directly from their Hindi text ([Moneycontrol](https://www.moneycontrol.com/artificial-intelligence/pocket-fm-now-lets-hindi-writers-in-india-create-ai-audio-series-in-a-single-click-article-13553178.html)). HeyGen guarantees exact speech only for a script the user proofreads in the target language ([HeyGen help](https://help.heygen.com/en/articles/10029081-how-to-get-started-with-video-translation)). One idea from the pivot is worth keeping in reserve. If a single stage struggles to understand Hindi, give it an internal-only English gloss of the story as extra context next to the original, never as a replacement ([LLM strategy notes](../research_notes/Hindi%20language%20support%20options/llm_language_strategy.md)). No study has tested a full multi-stage Hindi story pipeline, so this recommendation is assembled from component evidence, and the owner's own stories are the real test.

## Three GPU-server models, not the code, decide whether Hindi works

None of the speech, transcription or video adapters sends a language parameter. Whether Hindi works is decided entirely by what runs on the GPU server, and the repository never documents which languages those models support ([codebase text audit](../research_notes/Hindi%20language%20support%20options/codebase_text_speech_pipeline.md)). All three architectures hit these blockers equally.

### Breeze TTS 2 cannot speak Hindi, and its licence already blocks a monetized English channel

The open-weight Breeze TTS 2 that the owner self-hosts is made by BreezeBlue. Its model card describes "natural English and Chinese speech with a single model", with no Hindi. Only BreezeBlue's **hosted** `breeze-tts-2-multilingual` API lists Hindi among 51 languages ([Breeze-TTS-2 model card](https://huggingface.co/BreezeBlue/Breeze-TTS-2); [BreezeBlue docs](https://docs.breezeblue.ai/concepts/multilingual)). The codebase audit had guessed a MediaTek origin, but either way the conclusion is the same: no Hindi. The licence matters more than the language. Model weights and "self-hosted outputs are governed separately by the BreezeBlue Research and Non-Commercial License", and "a paid subscription does not grant commercial rights to the open-weight model or self-hosted outputs". Only output from the paid hosted platform "can be used commercially" ([Breeze-TTS-2 model card](https://huggingface.co/BreezeBlue/Breeze-TTS-2)). The app's own adapter notes the same restriction ([speech_synthesizer.py](../backend/app/providers/speech_synthesizer.py#L18)). **If the channel is monetized, the current English voices are probably already outside the licence.** This is a reading of the licence text, not legal advice. It turns the Hindi voice decision into a decision for both languages.

### Parakeet will probably return confident nonsense for Hindi, and the app will pay to retry

The transcriber sends only the audio, with the comment "There is no language field (Parakeet detects it)" ([transcriber.py](../backend/app/providers/transcriber.py#L8)). NVIDIA's `parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3` covers 25 European languages, **not Hindi**, and an NVIDIA maintainer confirmed it "doesn't receive or output language id" ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3); [NeMo issue #14799](https://github.com/NVIDIA-NeMo/NeMo/issues/14799)). If the server runs that model, Hindi audio will most likely come back as plausible-looking Latin or Cyrillic text rather than an error. That is an inference, and a five-minute test settles it. The consequences in the code are concrete. The story dialogue check compares the transcript of each H3 clip with the intended lines and regenerates the clip below an 80% match. **Each retry is "a new remote server job of 10 to 20 minutes"**, with up to two retries by default ([settings.py](../backend/app/core/settings.py#L576)). For explainers, word timings fall back to estimates, continuous takes are blocked, and recorded explainers are refused ([codebase text audit](../research_notes/Hindi%20language%20support%20options/codebase_text_speech_pipeline.md)). The only stopgap is setting `dialogue_match_threshold` to 0, which "accepts any speech". But it is one **global** setting, so it would also switch off the check for English projects. It should become per-language, a one-day change.

### H3 might carry Hindi through partial copy mode, so test it before redesigning

This is where the repository's design and the outside research disagree, and the honest answer is that it needs testing. In the pipeline's design, dialogue "is never invented by the model": the line is generated with TTS first and passed to H3 Ref2VA "as a reference in partial copy mode", where "the supplied speech is carried into the clip". H3's returned audio is then used as the shot's final sound, with no separate dialogue layer ([Path_ahead.md](../Path_ahead.md#L256); [Path_ahead.md](../Path_ahead.md#L1704)). The outside notes raise three doubts. H3's model card gives "stable support for 11 languages" that do not include Hindi, with "additional languages … supported to varying degrees" ([MiniMax-H3 model card](https://huggingface.co/MiniMaxAI/MiniMax-H3)). One weak source, a LinkedIn test, reported that for H3 "Hindi wasn't there" ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/dasachin_aivideo-generativeai-aifilmmaking-activity-7490735823517118464-hfxq)). And practitioners disagree on what reference audio does. Atlas Cloud describes it as a timbre anchor, with the spoken lines coming from the prompt ([Atlas Cloud](https://www.atlascloud.ai/blog/tips/minimax-h3-lip-sync-and-audio)). A Japanese-language practitioner found that passing the same TTS line did drive mouth timing, but still replaced H3's audio with the original TTS because H3's own speech "is not yet of practical quality". They also needed at least 0.9 seconds of leading silence ([ALTOLTA](https://note.com/altolta_428/n/n51b674a65091?hl=en)), which contrasts with the design's "H3 pads it automatically".

No source tested partial copy mode with Hindi, so the right move is a small experiment, not a redesign. If H3 carries the Hindi TTS faithfully, the design stays as it is and only the `<d>[English]` tag becomes `[Hindi]`; the parser already accepts any tag. If it does not, the fallback the outside notes suggest is to replace H3's voice with the TTS clip. My inference is that this is a real design change, because H3's track mixes the dialogue with the sound effects and ambience it generated. Replacing it means losing or rebuilding those sounds for every dialogue shot, roughly M to L in size. A dedicated lip-sync model (for example `sync/lipsync-v3` at $0.22 per second) is the last resort ([Atlas Cloud](https://www.atlascloud.ai/blog/tips/minimax-h3-lip-sync-and-audio)). Note the coupling here: the dialogue check is the tool that would notice whether H3 changed the words, so a Hindi recogniser is both the long-term quality gate and the measuring instrument for this test. For the first test, a Hindi-speaking listener can stand in for it.

## Decisions the owner must make, with the trade-offs

### Text-to-speech: choose one paid service that covers both languages

No open model matches Breeze's three modes (design a voice from a description, clone from a reference clip, direct the delivery) for Hindi under a clean commercial licence. In the only large human study of Indian-language TTS found (more than 120,000 votes from over 1,900 native raters), the ranking was **Gemini 2.5 Pro TTS first, ElevenLabs v3 and Cartesia Sonic 3 tied second, then Sarvam Bulbul v3, MiniMax Speech 2.8 HD and GPT-4o-mini TTS, with the open IndicF5 last on a 19% win rate** ([arXiv 2604.21481](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.21481v1)). Caution is warranted even at the top: Pocket FM, an audio-story platform whose biggest writer and listener base is Hindi, said in January 2026 that leading TTS providers "perform poorly" on Hindi dramatic dialogue ([YourStory](https://yourstory.com/2026/01/pocket-fm-hires-former-meta-ai-scientist-to-build-its-own-ai-models-for-storytelling)). A native listening test is therefore not optional. Cost is not the deciding factor. At list prices and an assumed (unverified for Hindi) 1,000 characters per minute, every option costs about $0.01–0.10 per finished minute, small next to video generation ([Hindi TTS notes](../research_notes/Hindi%20language%20support%20options/hindi_tts.md)).

| Option | Design / clone / direction in Hindi | List price | Word timings returned | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ElevenLabs v3/v4** | Voice Design (Hindi quality untested) / Instant clone from 30 s / audio tags like `[whispers]` | $80 per million characters ([pricing](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing/api)) | Per character ([API](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/text-to-speech/convert-with-timestamps)) | Cost; accent drift if voices are not Hindi-native |
| **MiniMax Speech 2.8** | Voice Design $3 per voice / clone $1.50 per voice / interjection tags; `language_boost: "Hindi"` | $60–100 per million ([pricing](https://platform.minimax.io/docs/pricing/overview)) | Per word ([T2A API](https://platform.minimax.io/docs/api-reference/speech-t2a-http)) | Hindi evidence is thin; same vendor as H3 (simpler accounts) |
| **BreezeBlue hosted multilingual** | Same three modes as today | Not gathered | Not gathered | Hindi quality unverified; probably the smallest integration |
| **Gemini 2.5 Pro / 3.8 Flash TTS** | Preset voices, delivery steered by prompt; no cloning found | About $0.01–0.03 per minute ([pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)) | Not documented | Few distinct character voices; best suited to narration |
| **Sarvam Bulbul v3** | Library voices, clone in beta, only pace and temperature controls | ₹3 per 1,000 characters ([pricing](https://www.sarvam.ai/api-pricing)) | Not found | Weak emotional direction for drama |
| Self-hosted open stack | Indic Parler-TTS (Apache-2.0) for design, Chatterbox-Multilingual-hi (MIT) for cloning | GPU time only | None; needs an aligner | Lower quality; Chatterbox unmaintained since August 2026 ([README](https://github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox)) |

My recommendation is to test **ElevenLabs v3 and MiniMax Speech 2.8**, with **BreezeBlue hosted** as a low-effort third. Then pick the one that wins the native listening test and survives the H3 test, and move English onto it at the same time to settle the licence. Choosing a service that returns word timings (ElevenLabs or MiniMax) also removes the need for a separate aligner when explainer captions come later. Two related choices follow. First, **design a separate Hindi voice per character**. ElevenLabs states that "the language is determined by the text, the accent and pronunciation is determined by the voice itself" ([ElevenLabs help](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/help-center/product/core-capabilities/text-to-speech/can-i-use-the-same-cloned-designed-voice-across-languages.md)). Chatterbox warns that a mismatched reference "may inherit the accent of the reference clip's language" ([README](https://github.com/resemble-ai/chatterbox)). Today every character sample reads a fixed English sentence ([voice_design_writer.py](../backend/app/services/voice_design_writer.py#L53)), so this means a Hindi sample line and a language on each saved voice. Second, **spell out numbers in Hindi words before TTS**. Open models garble raw digits, dates and amounts ([Sonex Labs](https://www.sonexlabs.com/blog/best-open-source-hindi-tts)). Even services that handle numbers internally benefit, because a deterministic spelling keeps the spoken words and the dialogue check in agreement.

### Transcription and alignment: route by language, and self-host unless sending audio out is acceptable

The app uses speech-to-text in three ways, and each needs a different tool. For **Hindi stories, only the first matters**: word timings are made only for explainers, and "a story never does" ([word_timings.py](../backend/app/services/word_timings.py#L276)).

| Use | What it needs | Recommended route | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dialogue check of H3 clips (stories) | Real recognition with word times | Self-hosted **IndicConformer 600M** (MIT; Hindi WER 13.2 on Vaani) ([HF](https://huggingface.co/ai4bharat/indic-conformer-600m-multilingual)), or hosted ElevenLabs Scribe or Deepgram Nova-3 | Avoid untuned Whisper: 29.1% WER on FLEURS Hindi ([SOTA2](https://www.sota2.com/research/sota/automatic-speech-recognition-on-fleurs-hindi-test)), and 121.4% WER on clips under 1 s because it invents words ([arXiv 2610.08847](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08847)) |
| Word timings of the app's own TTS (explainers) | Forced alignment: exact text known, find each word's start and end | Hindi wav2vec2 CTC aligner: **70.9 ms mean error, 43.4 ms after an offset correction**; NeMo Forced Aligner with IndicConformer is close behind at 79.5 ms ([arXiv 2610.08847](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08847)) | Unnecessary if the chosen TTS returns its own timings |
| User's own recordings (explainers) | Recognition first, then alignment, then matching to the script | Same recogniser plus aligner | Aligners end Hindi words about 93 ms early and start them about 36 ms late, so cuts need padding ([arXiv 2610.08847](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08847)) |

NVIDIA's only confirmed Hindi recogniser, Parakeet-RNNT-1.1B Multilingual, ships as a NIM container served over Riva gRPC, a different serving model from the current endpoint ([NVIDIA NIM card](https://build.nvidia.com/nvidia/parakeet-1_1b-rnnt-multilingual-asr/modelcard)). One aggregator reports that it needs about 50 GB of GPU memory ([llms.ninja](https://llms.ninja/nvidia/models/parakeet-1-1b-rnnt-multilingual-asr)), and its production licence terms were not verified. Sarvam has the best spontaneous-Hindi accuracy in one same-protocol comparison, but its API documentation says "word-level timestamps are not supported", even though its marketing page says they are ([Sarvam API](https://docs.sarvam.ai/api-reference/speech-to-text/transcribe)). Trust the documentation until tested. Whichever recogniser is chosen, the comparison step needs Hindi-aware matching. A local test of the current normaliser found that zero-width joiners split words, Devanagari digits never match ASCII digits, and हाँ (chandrabindu) does not match हां (anusvara) ([ASR notes](../research_notes/Hindi%20language%20support%20options/hindi_asr_alignment.md)). The script of English loanwords also matters a lot: one transcript scored **17% WER against an all-Devanagari reference and 0% against a mixed-script one** ([Sonex Labs](https://www.sonexlabs.com/blog/hindi-hinglish-speech-to-text-8khz-wer)). Self-hosting IndicConformer fits the app's self-hosted character and has a clean licence. Hosted recognisers are quicker to wire up, but they send story audio and the owner's voice recordings to third parties, which is a product decision rather than a technical one.

### Script: Devanagari first, Romanized Hindi later through a confirmed transliteration

Models handle Devanagari better than Romanized Hindi. In a 2026 study, Romanized input was the weakest for every model, **1–8 points below native script even for the top tier** ([Script Gap, EMNLP 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-industry.144.pdf)). Converting Romanized text back to Devanagari recovered most of the loss (GPT-4o rose from 75.3% to 80.1%) ([Hindu BusinessLine](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/business-tech/scripting-ideal-ai-output/article70396751.ece)). An LLM transliterates informal Romanized Hindi far better than the dedicated IndicXlit tool, at 21.5% against 45.5% word error ([IndoNLP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.indonlp-1.15.pdf)). Downstream tools also expect Devanagari: Sarvam's clone API rejects Romanized Indic text outright ([Sarvam clone API](https://docs.sarvam.ai/api-reference/voice-cloning/clone)). So launch with Devanagari only, normalise the story to one Unicode form (NFC) when it is saved, and warn when a "Hindi" story is mostly Latin letters. Later, accept Romanized input by running one LLM transliteration pass, showing the result to the user, and treating the confirmed Devanagari as the canonical story. One related choice needs a channel-wide rule: how English words inside Hindi are written. Netflix's Hindi guide transliterates them into Devanagari ([Netflix guide](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/115003196707-Hindi-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)), while many Hindi YouTube channels keep them in Latin. The choice affects how native-script voices pronounce them, how the dialogue check matches them and how captions look. Either way, keep the loanwords; do not "purify" them.

### Smaller choices that can wait for their phase

**Fonts:** bundle one family that has both Devanagari and Latin glyphs, so mixed lines like "भारत की GDP" need no font switching. Poppins is geometric like the current Montserrat headlines, and Noto Sans Devanagari or Mukta suit body text ([rendering notes](../research_notes/Hindi%20language%20support%20options/rendering_video_publishing.md)). Choose the font by project language so English videos keep their look. **Publishing:** upload Hindi as a separate video with `hi` as the title and audio language. That is fully automatable, whereas adding a Hindi audio track to an English video works only by hand in YouTube Studio ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13338784?hl=en)), and it would not fit lip-synced shots anyway. Hindi titles often mix scripts: in one niche study, 68% of 214 ranking titles joined both languages with a pipe ([Pretutorials](https://pretutorials.com/blog/how-india-searches-youtube-hinglish-data)). **Language model:** GLM-5.3 at maximum reasoning scored 69% with 25% stability on NC Bench's Hindi dialogue test, against 92% for Qwen 3.8 27B, while GLM 5.3 Flash scored 100% ([NC Bench](https://www.nc-bench.com/tests/language-writing/scenarios/hindi-character-dialogue)). Results swing that much between versions and runs, and the scoring method is not published, so treat this as a reason to test, not a verdict.

## What changes in the codebase: about twenty small fixes, a few medium ones, two large

Sizes follow the audits' scales: **S** is a day or less (a constant, a regex, a prompt line), **M** is a few days (several files, or a setting with a migration and UI), and **L** is a week or more (a new model, provider or pipeline stage). The last column says whether the item is needed for Hindi stories or only for explainers. Items marked "my estimate" are not from the audits.

**Where the rendering notes conflicted, the tested result wins.** The external rendering research inferred that the `python:3.13-slim` base image lacks FriBiDi, so Pillow would silently fall back to a basic layout and draw Devanagari wrongly. The codebase audit tested Pillow 12.3.0 inside the built backend image. It found RAQM active, FriBiDi 1.0.16 present (pulled in as a dependency of FFmpeg), and correctly shaped conjuncts and the left-side ि vowel sign once a Devanagari font was used ([rendering audit, experiments E1 and E5](../research_notes/Hindi%20language%20support%20options/codebase_rendering_output.md)). Because FriBiDi arrives only as a side effect of installing FFmpeg, the explicit RAQM request and start-up check are still worth the hour. On captions the two sources agree. The audit rendered Hindi through the current `subtitles` filter on FFmpeg 7.1.5 and got unshaped text even with a Devanagari font named, while the `ass` filter with `shaping=complex` drew it correctly. It also found that a Devanagari font placed in the fonts folder is not used as a fallback, so the caption style must name it ([rendering audit, E4](../research_notes/Hindi%20language%20support%20options/codebase_rendering_output.md)). The external notes explain why: until a May 2026 FFmpeg fix, the `subtitles` filter silently chose libass's simple shaper ([FFmpeg commit b08c9c5](https://github.com/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/commit/b08c9c5f533e7dced089d12d4526301cc200c16e)).

## Phased plan: a few days of tests, then Hindi stories, then explainers

**Phase 0 settles the four biggest unknowns cheaply, before any product code.** Each test below is sized in hours or days and decides something specific.

| Test | How | Effort | What it decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does the server's Parakeet handle Hindi? | Check which Parakeet model the server runs, then send 3–5 Hindi clips (any Hindi TTS or a phone recording) to `/v1/parakeet/transcribe` and read the output | Under an hour | Whether a Hindi recogniser is needed (expected: yes) |
| Can self-hosted Breeze speak Hindi? | Synthesize one Hindi line on the current server | Minutes | Confirms a new TTS is needed |
| Which Hindi voice service? | Run the same 1–2 minute excerpt through 2–3 candidates on trial tiers. Include emotional dialogue, numbers and dates, English loanwords, nukta words (ज़, फ़) and a full 1,000-character chunk. Native listeners rank them | 1–2 days | The TTS choice, for both languages |
| Does H3 carry Hindi in partial copy mode? | Make 2–3 dialogue shots with `<d>[Hindi] …</d>` and the Hindi TTS clip as reference, in Devanagari and once in Romanized form, with and without about 0.9 s of leading silence. A Hindi listener compares H3's audio with the TTS word by word and checks the lip sync | About a day plus GPU time | Whether the design in `Path_ahead.md` holds, or the voice-track fallback is needed |
| GLM-5.3 or Qwen3.8? | Put 5–10 real Hindi stories through Story Bible, scene discovery, events and speech units, with the danda and normalisation fixes applied. Measure scene boundaries, speaker attribution, copy-check retries, the quality and register of Hindi titles, and tokens and cost per story. Check whether the 48,000-token and 45,000-character limits hold | 2–3 days | Model routing; whether event speech should be sliced by code |
| What do the licences allow? | Read the Breeze terms, the H3 community licence and the shortlisted TTS terms | Hours | Whether English moves to the new TTS now |

**Phase 1 lays foundations that are safe for English and useful before any Hindi audio exists**, in roughly one to two weeks. It adds the project language column and the Story Bible language, normalisation on save, the danda fixes, the normalised `<d>` check and the `[Hindi]` tag, Hindi display titles for scenes, per-language words-per-minute and dialogue thresholds, the transcriber language field, Hindi YouTube metadata and hashtags, the explicit Pillow shaping engine, and a bundled Devanagari font for thumbnails. Until Phase 2, the Hindi dialogue check stays off, and a Hindi-speaking reviewer checks clips by ear.

**Phase 2 produces Hindi stories end to end.** It covers the new TTS adapter (moving English onto it too if the licence check says so), per-language voices with Hindi sample lines, Hindi number spelling before TTS, and the Hindi recogniser with Hindi-aware matching, after which the dialogue check is switched back on. The H3 fallback is built only if Phase 0 showed it is needed. The exit test is one complete Hindi story video, approved by a native listener and uploaded as a separate video with Hindi metadata.

**Phase 3 brings explainers to Hindi.** That means burned captions (the `ass` filter, the font, line heights, width-based limits, danda breaks, no forced capitals) and word timings from an aligner or from the TTS's own timestamps, with padded splice points for recordings. It also covers Hindi on-screen text in the graphics writers, Hindi number words in the checks, लाख/करोड़ number display, and evidence capture with a Hindi locale plus a test on real Hindi sources.

**Phase 4 is optional.** It covers Romanized story intake through a confirmed transliteration, bilingual titles with a second caption track, Hindi dub tracks on English narration-only videos through YouTube Studio, and Hindi songs. ACE-Step 1.5 accepts `vocal_language="hi"`, but one LoRA author notes that the base model "was trained mostly on Western music" ([RagaLoRA](https://huggingface.co/veeceey/RagaLoRA-indian-music-ace-step)).

## Conclusion

The most urgent finding in a Hindi investigation turned out not to be about Hindi. The voice engine behind every current English video is licensed for non-commercial use only. So the first decision, choosing a commercially licensed voice service, should be made once for both languages, and the Hindi listening test is a natural way to make it. The second insight is that a design choice made for English reliability, treating the author's words as untouchable and cutting them out in code, is also the right multilingual design. The English pivot would quietly undo it, which is the strongest argument against translating at the edges.

The remaining uncertainty is empirical, and cheap to resolve. Sequencing stories before explainers means the expensive pieces (captions, alignment, Hindi number checks) wait until the cheap tests show that Hindi voice, recognition and H3 behave, and a Hindi-speaking reviewer can stand in for the automatic dialogue check meanwhile. If H3's partial copy mode carries Hindi faithfully and one hosted voice passes a native listening test, Hindi stories are a matter of weeks of mostly small changes. If H3 does not, the project gains one real design change in how dialogue audio is assembled, and it is better to learn that from three test shots than from a finished pipeline.

## Plain-words glossary

| Term | Meaning here |
|---|---|
| TTS (text-to-speech) | The voice engine that reads lines aloud; Breeze TTS 2 today |
| ASR, recogniser, transcription | Speech-to-text; Parakeet today. Used to check that H3 clips say the right words |
| Forced alignment | Given audio and the exact text, finding when each word starts and ends; needed for word-by-word captions and cutting recordings |
| CTC aligner | A common kind of alignment model; it tends to mark word ends slightly early |
| Verbatim chain | Scene text, then event text, then speech unit, then H3 dialogue tag, each required to be an exact copy of the author's words |
| Devanagari | The script Hindi is written in (हिन्दी) |
| Romanized Hindi, Hinglish | Hindi typed in Latin letters ("kya haal hai"), and Hindi mixed with English words |
| Danda (।) | The Hindi full stop; the code does not yet treat it as a sentence end |
| Matra, virama, nukta | Vowel signs (ि, ी), the mark that joins consonants (्), and the dot below letters like ज़ |
| Unicode normalisation (NFC) | Making sure the same letter is always stored the same way; क़ can be one character or two |
| Token, token tax | The units a language model reads and bills; the same Hindi content can take about 3× the tokens of English on GLM |
| Shaping (RAQM, HarfBuzz) | The step that joins and reorders Devanagari letters for display; without it, ि appears on the wrong side and conjuncts break |
| libass, ASS filter | The subtitle renderer FFmpeg uses to burn captions into video |
| Tofu | Empty boxes drawn when a font has no Devanagari letters |
| Ref2VA, partial copy mode | H3's mode that makes video and audio from reference images and audio; in partial copy mode the supplied speech is meant to be carried into the clip |
| WER, CER | Word and character error rates for speech recognition; lower is better |
| Grapheme cluster | What a reader sees as one letter, even when it is several characters, such as क्ष |
