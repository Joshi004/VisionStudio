# Using real people's video sound bites accurately and fairly in AI-narrated explainers (and on YouTube)

> Research notes compiled 21 Nov 2026. **Not legal advice.** Copyright exceptions (US fair use, UK/India fair dealing) and defamation law differ by country and are decided case by case; a lawyer should review anything high-risk (allegation segments, monetised use of third-party footage). Pages were fetched live on 21 Nov 2026 unless marked older.

## 1. Journalism standards for editing someone's recorded words

### Takeaway
Every major standards body allows shortening and "cleaning up" a sound bite but forbids edits that change meaning. The video equivalent of an ellipsis is a visible transition (a wipe, a flash or a camera change), never a seamless join. Joining remarks spoken at different times into what looks like one sentence ("composite" quotes) is treated as a serious breach. Archive material has to be identifiable as archive, and the circumstances of a quote (when it was said, what question it answered) should be disclosed when they matter.

### Cited Findings
- **AP**: "The video or audio editing of quotations or soundbites must not alter the speaker's meaning. Internal editing of audio soundbites of newsmakers is not permitted. Shortened soundbites by cutaway or other video transition are permitted as long as the speaker's meaning is not altered or misconstrued." Sound edits are allowed only in narrow cases (for example a technical failure) and only "after approval by a senior editorial manager." — [AP Statement of News Values and Principles (via Accountable Journalism)](https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/news-values-and-principles); same text at [News Leaders Association mirror](https://members.newsleaders.org/resources-ethics-ap). *Possibly older: these mirrors carry a long-standing version, and the current AP text was not verified directly.*
- **AP**: stories should give "information about the setting in which a quotation was obtained — for example, a press conference, phone interview or hallway conversation"; "The source's affect and body language — perhaps a smile or deprecatory gesture — is sometimes as important as the quotation itself." — [AP via Accountable Journalism](https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/news-values-and-principles)
- **PBS Standards**: "For video, a producer who elects to immediately jump from one portion of a person's remarks to a later portion should strongly consider including an audio or visual cue (such as a flash, wipe, or different camera) to signal to the audience that the soundbite is not, in fact, one seamless quote. In contrast, seamlessly editing together two separate statements while showing B-roll would generally not be sufficiently transparent." — [PBS Standards, "Handle Interview Edits with Care"](https://www.pbs.org/standards/blogs/standards-memos/handling-quotations/)
- **PBS**: "It is not appropriate to take disparate phrases or sentence fragments spoken over the course of an interview or speech to create a composite sentence that is presented as one single quotation… The subject, verb, and object of a sentence should not be broken apart or substituted with other parts of an interview or speech." Its suggested test: "Does this edit preserve sufficient context to ensure that the audience is not misled… and would not feel deceived if subsequently reviewing the entire footage?" — [PBS Standards](https://www.pbs.org/standards/blogs/standards-memos/handling-quotations/)
- **PBS** (legal angle): it cites *Masson v. New York Magazine* (1991), where the US Supreme Court held that "a 'fabricated quotation' can give rise to a claim for defamation, as can 'an exact quotation out of context.'" — [PBS Standards](https://www.pbs.org/standards/blogs/standards-memos/handling-quotations/)
- **BBC 3.4.25**: news and factual content should not normally "inter-cut shots and sequences if the resulting juxtaposition of material leads to a materially misleading impression of events. Commentary and editing must never be used to give the audience a materially misleading impression of events or a contribution." — [BBC Editorial Guidelines, Section 3 Accuracy](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)
- **BBC 3.4.32 (Archive)**: "Archive material should not be used in a way that materially misleads the audience about a situation, events or the subject being depicted… where appropriate it should be made clear to audiences that the material is archive." — [BBC Editorial Guidelines](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)
- **BBC 3.4.19**: "Omitting significant details may lead to inaccuracy. Audiences should be provided with the relevant facts, complete information and context." **3.4.16**: producers "may need to clarify the nature of some content by labelling (for example, verbally, in text or with visual or audio cues)." — [BBC Editorial Guidelines](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)
- **BBC 3.4.26–3.4.29 (synthetic content and AI)**: digital creation or manipulation, including synthetic content, must not "distort the meaning of events, alter the impact of genuine material or otherwise materially mislead"; "Any use of AI by the BBC must be transparent and clear to the audience… it may be helpful to explain not just that AI has been used but how and why"; any product "that uses AI to distribute or curate content is considered to be an editorial experience and therefore subject to editorial approval and human oversight." **3.4.18**: "Content producers using any automated analysis of data should give it a sense check." — [BBC Editorial Guidelines](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)
- **Ofcom Broadcasting Code**: Rule 7.1 says broadcasters "must avoid unjust or unfair treatment of individuals or organisations". Practice 7.6: "When a programme is edited, contributions should be represented fairly." Practice 7.8: "re-use of material, i.e. use of material originally filmed or recorded for one purpose and then used in a programme for another purpose… does not create unfairness. This applies both to material obtained from others and the broadcaster's own material." Practice 7.9 applies "including programmes examining past events." — [Ofcom Broadcasting Code, Section 7 Fairness](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/tv-radio-and-on-demand/broadcast-codes/broadcast-code/section-seven-fairness) (page last updated 19 Nov 2026; practice wording dates from 2021)
- **NPR Ethics Handbook**: "An actuality from an interviewee or speaker should reflect accurately what that person was asked or was responding to. If we use tape or material from an earlier story, we clearly identify it as such. We tell listeners about the circumstances of an interview if that information is pertinent (such as the time the interview took place…)… The burden is on NPR journalists to ensure that our use of such material is true to the meaning the interviewee or speaker intended." — [NPR News Code of Ethics (mirror at Reference.org)](https://reference.org/news/Talk+radio/S9MFrD4w) *(mirror of an older NPR code; current NPR handbook wording not verified)*
- **NPR Training (2025)**: removing ums and stumbles is "fine, as long as you are not removing something that suggested the interviewee was reluctant or unable to respond to a question." Cutting digressions or whole Q&As is acceptable "as long as essential context remains and the meaning is not altered in any way." Editors noted "in radio reporting there is no ellipsis." NPR "does not clean up presidential remarks" (per the search summary of the same page). — [NPR Training, "How to decide what to cut (or not) in an interview"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-65686/how-to-decide-what-to-cut-or-not-in-an-interview)
- **Public media ethics guide (2004)**: "Consider… whether eliminating a pause within a soundbite, or making an internal edit, would alter the listener's perception. Ask yourself whether sources would recognize themselves in their soundbites." — [Independence and Integrity II (Public Media Integrity)](https://publicmediaintegrity.org/wp-content/uploads/EthicsGuide010604.pdf) *(older, 2004)*
- **IPSO Editors' Code (UK press, 2025 version) Clause 1**: "The Press must take care not to publish inaccurate, misleading or distorted information or images"; "must distinguish clearly between comment, conjecture and fact." — [IPSO Editors' Code of Practice](https://www.ipso.co.uk/editors-code-of-practice/)

### Inferences
- For the app, "trim at word boundaries" is not enough. The standards turn on **meaning units** (a complete sentence or thought, plus the question it answers), so trims should snap to sentence or clause boundaries and check what comes immediately before and after the cut.
- A single, contiguous, unaltered segment is the safe default. Any internal edit (two segments from the same source) should be blocked or need a visible transition and explicit human approval, mirroring AP's "senior editorial manager" rule.
- An explainer built from archival clips is "re-use for another purpose" in Ofcom 7.8 terms, so the date and context of each clip carry more weight than in live news.
- Ofcom and IPSO bind UK broadcasters and press, not YouTube creators. BBC, AP, NPR and PBS are voluntary in-house codes. They are still the best available yardstick for "defensible", and courts and platforms look at the same harms (defamation, misleading content).

### Gaps
- The **SPJ Code of Ethics** page (spj.org/ethicscode.asp) returned 404 and could not be checked. Its wording on "never deliberately distort facts or context, including visual information" is from memory and unverified.
- The **Reuters Handbook of Journalism** section on editing quotes and soundbites was not retrieved.
- IMPRESS Standards Code was not retrieved.

## 2. Out-of-context clipping: well-known controversies and what they teach

### Takeaway
The two reference cases are Shirley Sherrod (2010) and BBC Panorama's Trump edit (aired 2024, exposed November 2025). Sherrod shows how cutting before a story's turn ("but then I realised…") inverts its meaning. Panorama shows how joining two remarks made 54 minutes apart, with no visual signal and with suggestive B-roll after them, makes a person "say" something they never said. The Panorama case cost the BBC's director-general and head of news their jobs.

### Cited Findings
- **Sherrod**: a clip of "several minutes of her roughly 45-minute speech" was posted by Andrew Breitbart, labelled "racist", and she was forced to resign from USDA. The full tape showed "the climax, not to mention the moral, of Sherrod's tale had been edited out": she went on to help the white farmer save his farm. The story described events 24 years earlier. — [FactCheck.org](https://www.factcheck.org/2010/07/shirley-sherrods-contextual-nightmare/)
- The edited clip ran two and a half minutes. Sherrod said the excerpts came from "a longer story she told about overcoming prejudice and learning… that her job was to help poor people regardless of their race." — [NYT The Lede](https://archive.nytimes.com/thelede.blogs.nytimes.com/2010/07/21/longer-video-of-controversial-speech-released-by-n-a-a-c-p/)
- The NAACP had condemned her based on the edited video. After reviewing the full 43-minute speech it said it was mistaken. — [NPR](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2010/07/20/128657452/naacp-posts-entire-shirley-sherrod-video); the White House apologised — [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-us-canada-10716237)
- **Panorama / Trump**: the programme showed Trump saying "We're going to walk down to the Capitol... and I'll be there with you. And we fight. We fight like hell." "The two sections that were stitched together were originally more than 50 minutes apart." BBC director-general Tim Davie and News CEO Deborah Turness resigned on 9 Nov 2025. — [BBC News](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c3vn25d5dq7o)
- The leaked Prescott memo said the first half came 15 minutes into the speech and the second "54 minutes later", and that the splice "materially misled viewers." The clip "was followed by video footage of the Proud Boys marching towards Congress", which "created the impression Trump's supporters had taken up his 'call-to-arms'", although they had marched before he spoke. A BBC executive's defence was quoted as "It's normal practice to edit speeches into short form clips." — [Telegraph, memo in full](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/2025/11/06/read-devastating-internal-bbc-memo-in-full/)
- The Telegraph reported "foreboding music in the background", "no screen wipe or caption over the footage to signal to viewers that it had been edited or was out of sequence", and said three separate parts were joined. — [Telegraph](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/2025/11/03/bbc-report-reveals-bias-donald-trump/) *(the BBC reports two parts and the Telegraph three; the sources differ on the count)*
- BBC chair Samir Shah accepted an "error of judgement": the edit "gave the impression of a 'direct call for action'". BBC News had said the purpose was "to convey the message of the speech". — [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c4gw001kw97o)
- In the omitted context, Trump said "peacefully and patriotically make your voices heard." — [Telegraph](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/2025/11/03/bbc-report-reveals-bias-donald-trump/); see also [Guardian explainer](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2025/nov/10/why-bbc-resignations-trump-speech-edit)
- **Partial quotes are not inherently misleading**: selecting a phrase is legitimate if the result is "still being true to the essence of the source's statement." — [News Literacy Matters](https://newsliteracymatters.com/2019/10/10/q-arent-partial-quotations-taken-out-of-context-and-therefore-unreliable/)
- An IBA checklist cites a survey in which "one person in three said important information was left out of a story and one in five complained that his or her interview was taken out of context." It advises adding context in narration where needed. — [IBA Newsroom](https://www.ibanewsroom.org/newsroom-tips/multi-media-reporter-checklist-for-accuracy/) *(the survey itself is not identified; treat the statistic as weak)*

### Inferences
Tests for whether a short excerpt preserves meaning, drawn from the cases above:
1. **Turn test (Sherrod)**: does the speaker go on to qualify, reverse or "land" the point within the next few sentences? Words such as "but", "however", "that's when I realised", "I was wrong" or "of course, that's not true" after the out-point are red flags.
2. **Voice test**: is the speaker stating their own view, or quoting or voicing someone else ("people told me…", "critics say…", "the old me thought…")?
3. **Splice test (Panorama)**: is the clip one continuous span? If it is not, is the join visibly signalled, and do the joined parts keep their original sense?
4. **Adjacency test (Panorama B-roll)**: does the material placed after or around the clip (narration, B-roll, music) create a causal or temporal implication the facts don't support?
5. **Full-footage test (PBS)**: would a viewer who later watched the whole source feel deceived?
6. **Recognition test**: would the speaker recognise their own meaning in it?
- For automation, tests 1, 2 and 3 can be partly checked by machine (transcript context window plus heuristics or an LLM classifier). Tests 4, 5 and 6 need human judgment, informed by context the tool surfaces.

### Gaps
- Other well-known cases (for example selectively edited undercover videos, or edited clips in political ads) were not researched in this pass.
- No academic study was found that quantifies how many seconds of context make an excerpt "safe". This is an editorial judgment, not a number.

## 3. Speaker verification and provenance (re-uploads, impersonators, parody, deepfakes, dubbing)

### Takeaway
Fact-checking practice assumes the copy you found is not the original. It traces the earliest upload, assesses the uploading account, and corroborates date and place with several independent signals, because no single signal (including deepfake detectors) is conclusive. For a pipeline that pulls clips from YouTube, this means preferring official first-party channels, recording provenance metadata, checking that the voice and face in the clip belong to the named person, and rejecting dubbed, translated or "AI" uploads.

### Cited Findings
- "the version you first see [is] unlikely to be the original. Videos may be spliced, diced and reposted with different context… you always start from the assumption that a video has been 'scraped' or duplicated." To find the original: search keywords from the description, sort by upload date, and reverse-search the thumbnail with Google Images or TinEye. — [Verification Handbook, ch. 5 "Verifying Video" (DataJournalism.com)](https://datajournalism.com/read/handbook/verification-1/verifying-video/5-verifying-video) *(older, first edition c. 2014)*
- Questions to ask about the uploader: is the account known and reliable; where is it based; is the logo consistent; "Does the uploader 'scrape' videos from news organizations and other YouTube accounts?" "one piece of evidence alone is insufficient to verify a video." — [Verification Handbook ch. 5](https://datajournalism.com/read/handbook/verification-1/verifying-video/5-verifying-video)
- YouTube date stamps may use Pacific time, and supporting evidence should predate the example. — [GIJN guide to verifying digital content](https://gijn.org/stories/a-guide-to-verifying-digital-content-in-emergencies/)
- **Deepfakes**: "A perfect deepfake detection system will never exist"; "the absence of evidence that something was tampered with will not be conclusive proof." Recommended approach: look for glitches (least robust), apply standard provenance verification (reverse-search frames, check source, date and motivation), use forensic tools such as InVID as "signals to complement" verification, and "contact the person or people featured in the video." Real content is also increasingly dismissed as fake. — [Verification Handbook 3 (EJC, 2020)](https://files.ejc.net/production/resources/2020/verification-handbook-3-for-disinformation-and-media-manipulation/Verification-Handbook-3.pdf) *(older, 2020; detection tools have moved on since)*
- Research cited in the same handbook found "people could not reliably detect current forms of lip movement modification", the technique used to match a mouth to a new audio track. — [Verification Handbook 3](https://files.ejc.net/production/resources/2020/verification-handbook-3-for-disinformation-and-media-manipulation/Verification-Handbook-3.pdf)
- **BBC 3.4.6 and 3.4.10–3.4.13**: digital material "must be checked and verified"; if it can't be verified, "say so and attribute"; user-generated material "should always be clearly identified"; "check who is running the website or confirm with an individual or organisation that the material is genuine"; "Material that content producers did not gather themselves should be attributed." — [BBC Editorial Guidelines](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)
- **BBC 3.4.14**: be "reluctant to use video and audio news releases or other material… supplied by outside parties who may have vested interests" (this includes companies); where used, it "should be clearly labelled. It must be stated who supplied it." — [BBC Editorial Guidelines](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)
- **AP**: "Quotes from one language to another must be translated faithfully. If appropriate, we should note the language spoken." — [AP via Accountable Journalism](https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/news-values-and-principles)
- **YouTube** may auto-label content as AI if it contains C2PA metadata or its systems detect AI generation or alteration. — [YouTube Help: Disclosing use of GenAI content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)

### Inferences
- Keynote clips (Nvidia GTC, Computex) usually exist on an official first-party channel. The pipeline should prefer that upload and treat third-party re-uploads, "full speech" compilations and fan channels as lower-trust sources that need human sign-off. That covers the "earliest or original upload" step.
- Specific failure modes to check for:
  - an interpreter or translator speaking over the person;
  - YouTube auto-dubbed or alternate audio tracks;
  - a host or reporter *reading* the person's quote;
  - a moderator or audience member speaking during the matched words;
  - a lookalike or impersonator, or a parody;
  - AI voice or lip-sync fakes.
  Speaker diarisation, a face-on-screen check during the spoken words, and comparison against a reference voice from a verified official source are reasonable automated signals. None is conclusive on its own, which matches the handbook's "body of evidence" principle.
- Because the app matches clips by transcript, auto-captions are a risk: an ASR error can produce a false "match". Exact-quote claims should be confirmed by a second transcription or by human listening.

### Gaps
- Current (2026) guidance from **Bellingcat** and **AFP Fact Check** on YouTube provenance was not retrieved. First Draft's guides are referenced through the Verification Handbook only. I believe First Draft wound down around 2022, but did not verify this.
- Whether YouTube exposes C2PA or "altered content" label data through its public API was not checked.

## 4. Quote fidelity: when the script quote differs from what was said on video

### Takeaway
Quotation marks, or presenting a clip as "he said", promise the exact words. AP does not alter quotes even to fix grammar: an imperfect quote is either paraphrased faithfully, clearly marked as paraphrase, or dropped. NPR applies the same rule to audio: verbatim words must be signalled as the source's own, and paraphrase still needs attribution. If narration says "Huang said X" and the clip that follows says Y, the audience will take X as a direct quote, so the two must match or be clearly separated.

### Cited Findings
- **AP**: "We do not alter quotations, even to correct grammatical errors or word usage. If a quotation is flawed because of grammar or lack of clarity, the writer must be able to paraphrase in a way that is completely true to the original quote. If a quote's meaning is too murky to be paraphrased accurately, it should not be used. Ellipses should be used rarely." — [AP via Accountable Journalism](https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/news-values-and-principles)
- AP Stylebook (as quoted in 2026 training material): "Ellipses should be used rarely and must not alter the speaker's meaning." — [MNA 2026 AP Style handout](https://mna.org/assets/2026/02/MNA-2026-Polish-Your-AP-Style-Handouts-Doug-Hanneman.pdf)
- **NPR**: "whenever we present someone's words verbatim in text, we encase them within quotes or, in an audio report, make it clear that we are using the source's wording. If we paraphrase for space or clarity, we transparently credit the source of the ideas. And we don't lightly edit quotes just to avoid putting them in quotes." — [NPR Ethics: Honesty](https://www.npr.org/about-npr/688367308/honesty)
- **PBS**: "If a producer believes that a person's words can be better stated another way, then a narrator could be used in video to summarize the speaker's remarks." — [PBS Standards](https://www.pbs.org/standards/blogs/standards-memos/handling-quotations/)
- **IPSO Clause 1(iv)**: distinguish "comment, conjecture and fact." — [IPSO](https://www.ipso.co.uk/editors-code-of-practice/)
- *Masson*: quotation marks signal the speaker's own words, and fabricated or out-of-context quotes can be defamatory (US). — [PBS Standards](https://www.pbs.org/standards/blogs/standards-memos/handling-quotations/)

### Inferences
Proposed fidelity classes for the app. These are my own design, built on the AP and NPR rules above:
- **Exact**: the clip's words match the script quote after normalising case, punctuation and filler words ("um", "uh", repeated words). The script may present it as a direct quote.
- **Near**: the same words with small differences (contractions, a dropped filler, a "gonna"/"going to" spelling). Prefer rewriting the script to the clip's exact words; AP permits spelling normalisation of dialect but not grammatical rewriting.
- **Paraphrase**: same meaning, different words. Narration must be marked as paraphrase (for example "Huang has argued that…", with no quotation marks on-screen). The clip can follow to let the person speak, but the on-screen caption must be the verbatim transcript.
- **Mismatch**: different meaning, or the words are not found. Block the clip.

Two further rules follow. On-screen captions for the real clip must always be verbatim, including "[inaudible]" where needed, and never the script's version. Translations must be labelled as translations.

### Gaps
- No standard was found that sets a numeric word-match threshold; any threshold (for example 95% token match) is a product choice.
- The current AP Stylebook (2026) quotation entry was seen only as quoted in a training handout, not in the primary text.

## 5. Sensitive subjects: allegations, scandal, juxtaposition, right of reply (Jensen Huang)

### Takeaway
The Huang story is real, recent and still moving. He resigned as Nvidia CEO on 12 Nov 2026 after reports of affairs, one with a woman US intelligence reportedly suspects of being a Chinese intelligence asset, which Beijing denies. Parts of the reporting are unconfirmed, an investigation is ongoing, and congressional appearances are scheduled for 1–2 Dec 2026. The standards require separating established fact (resignation, his admission of infidelity, the board's statements) from allegation (the spy link, other relationships). They also require attributing allegations to their reporters, including the subject's response, and not placing an unrelated clip of Huang next to allegation narration in a way that implies a response or admission.

### Cited Findings
- **Facts as reported**: Huang "has resigned from his top post… after reports of the tech titan's extramarital affairs." The NYT reported an affair with "a businesswoman U.S. intelligence suspects to be a Chinese spy, which Beijing denies," and a separate affair with an executive who reported to him. The resignation was announced Thursday, "effective immediately." — [AP](https://apnews.com/article/nvidia-jensen-huang-resigns-ceo-affairs-314714d463cf578cbb0dbd49de903107)
- Nvidia's board statement on the departure "did not mention the spying scandal and personal conduct revelations." Earlier, Huang "admitted he had been unfaithful to his wife, Lori, without referring to Alice Huang directly." Wachtell, Lipton was appointed to investigate. — [Guardian, 12 Nov 2026](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/12/nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-resigns)
- US Attorney General Todd Blanche said "the Justice Department has not yet reached a conclusion" on whether Alice Huang was a spy. Reuters "could not immediately verify" a CNBC/Tesari report about her business stakes. The saga began with an October 2025 NYT report. — [Reuters via Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-resigns-in-blow-to-ai-boom-5021980)
- The board chair said Huang "voluntarily resigned" after disclosing "personal behavior inconsistent with the company's code of conduct" and that the board "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO." Huang's statement: "I did not live up to the standards expected of everyone at NVIDIA, and I have made serious mistakes in my personal life… I own my mistakes completely." NPR "has not independently confirmed those reports." Closed-door House committee sessions are set for 1–2 Dec. — [NPR, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.npr.org/2026/11/17/nx-s1-6044213/jensen-huang-nvidia-congress)
- The independent board members plan to continue investigating despite the resignation. — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/nvidia-investigation-jensen-huang-continue-departure-sources-say-rcna609136)
- **Right of reply**: "If a programme alleges wrongdoing or incompetence or makes other significant allegations, those concerned should normally be given an appropriate and timely opportunity to respond" (Ofcom 7.11). Absent parties' views "must be done in a fair manner" (7.13). Facts must not be "presented, disregarded or omitted in a way that is unfair" (7.9). — [Ofcom Section 7](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/tv-radio-and-on-demand/broadcast-codes/broadcast-code/section-seven-fairness)
- **AP**: "We must make significant efforts to reach anyone who may be portrayed in a negative way… If we don't reach the parties involved, we must explain in the story what efforts were made." — [AP via Accountable Journalism](https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/news-values-and-principles)
- **IPSO 1(iii)**: "A fair opportunity to reply to significant inaccuracies should be given, when reasonably called for." — [IPSO](https://www.ipso.co.uk/editors-code-of-practice/)
- **BBC 3.4.22**: allegations from anonymous sources should be scripted to explain "the nature of the allegation" and "that the allegation is being made by an anonymous source and not the BBC"; where uncorroborated, consider telling the audience. **3.4.33**: content prepared in advance "must be checked to make sure it has not been overtaken by events." — [BBC Editorial Guidelines](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)
- **Juxtaposition**: BBC 3.4.25 prohibits inter-cutting that creates "a materially misleading impression" — [BBC](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines); the Panorama B-roll placement is the concrete example — [Telegraph memo](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/2025/11/06/read-devastating-internal-bbc-memo-in-full/)
- **Ofcom 7.14** (celebrities): material involving "those in the public eye" should not be used without public-interest justification "if it is likely to result in unjustified public ridicule or personal distress." — [Ofcom Section 7](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/tv-radio-and-on-demand/broadcast-codes/broadcast-code/section-seven-fairness)

### Inferences
- The highest-risk pattern for this project is narration such as "reports alleged an affair with a suspected spy…" followed by a keynote clip of Huang saying something like "we made mistakes" or "trust is everything", which was actually about GPUs. That clip would imply an admission or response. Clips placed inside allegation segments should be limited to (a) his actual statements on the matter, labelled with date and venue, or (b) clearly labelled neutral identification footage ("Huang at GTC, March 2024 — archival"), with no clip whose words could be read as commenting on the allegation.
- In scripts, keep three tiers: **established** (resigned 12 Nov 2026; admitted infidelity; board statements), **reported or alleged** (attribute to NYT, Reuters and others, and note "not independently confirmed" where outlets say so), and **denied** (Beijing's denial; Megaspeed's statement per NPR). Include Huang's own published statement as the reply. A YouTube creator is not bound by Ofcom, but this is the defensible standard and reduces defamation exposure.
- The story is moving (possible reinstatement push, 1–2 Dec hearings, investigation outcomes), so add a pre-publish re-check, a visible "as of [date]" line, and a corrections process.
- If the script adds any new claim not already in mainstream reporting, ask Nvidia or Huang's representatives for comment before publishing, and get legal review.

### Gaps
- Defamation law specifics for repeating published allegations (US, UK, India) were not researched; get legal advice.

## 6. YouTube-specific: fair use and fair dealing, Content ID, reused content, AI disclosure, creator practice

### Takeaway
Short commentary clips from keynotes and news have a good fair use argument in the US (transformative, factual, small amounts), and narrower fair dealing gateways in the UK and India (criticism or review, or reporting current events, with acknowledgement). Credits and "no infringement intended" disclaimers do not create fair use. Content ID can still claim even a few seconds, and disputes are the remedy. YouTube's reused content policy is separate from copyright and judges the whole channel: AI-narrated explainers must show "significant original commentary" and must not look mass-produced or templated. AI narration over real footage does not by itself trigger YouTube's synthetic-content disclosure, but any AI alteration that makes a real person appear to say something does. **Not legal advice.**

### Cited Findings
**US fair use**
- The four factors are purpose and character, nature of the work ("primarily factual works is more likely to be considered fair use"), amount ("if what's borrowed is considered the 'heart' of the work, sometimes even a small sampling may not be considered fair use"), and market effect. "Courts decide fair use cases according to the facts of each unique case." — [YouTube Help: Fair use on YouTube](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9783148?hl=en)
- "Giving credit to the owner of a copyrighted work won't by itself turn a non-transformative copy of their material into fair use… 'I do not own' don't automatically mean you're making fair use"; "There aren't any magic words." — [YouTube Help: Fair use](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9783148?hl=en)
- **CMSI Documentary Statement**: quoting works "to illustrate an argument or point" is generally fair use if the material "is properly attributed", quotations are "drawn from a range of different sources", each is "no longer than is necessary to achieve the intended effect", and it is "not employed merely in order to avoid the cost or inconvenience of shooting equivalent footage." Commercial uses can be fair. Fair use also considers "Whether the user acted reasonably and in good faith, in light of general practice in his or her particular field." For historical sequences, the project should "not rely predominantly or disproportionately on any single source." — [CMSI Documentary Filmmakers' Statement of Best Practices in Fair Use](https://cmsimpact.org/code/documentary-filmmakers-statement-of-best-practices-in-fair-use/) *(older, November 2005; still widely cited, but a newer edition may exist)*

**Content ID**
- A claim "will keep you from monetizing the video, even if you only use a few seconds." "Automated systems like Content ID can't decide fair use"; creators can dispute, then appeal or counter-notify. While a dispute is pending, "the video will still monetize until the dispute is resolved", and accrued earnings are then paid to the winning party. — [YouTube Help: Fair use](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9783148?hl=en)
- YouTube may withhold earnings for up to 90 days for Content ID claims and rights disputes. — [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)

**Outside the US**
- "In the EU, the use must fit into specific categories, such as quotation, criticism, review, caricature, parody, and pastiche. Other countries/regions have a concept called fair dealing." YouTube "consider[s] local rules when we review copyright removal requests." — [YouTube Help: Fair use](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9783148?hl=en)
- **UK**: fair dealing for "criticism, review or quotation is allowed for any type of copyright work"; reporting current events is allowed for any work except photographs; "a sufficient acknowledgement will be required." There is no statutory definition of fair dealing: the test is "how would a fair-minded and honest person have dealt with the work?" Courts look at market substitution and whether the amount taken was "reasonable and appropriate." — [GOV.UK: Exceptions to copyright](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/exceptions-to-copyright)
- **India**: Section 52(1)(a) covers private use, "criticism or review, whether of that work or of any other work", and "the reporting of current events and current affairs". There is "no fixed threshold… no 'safe harbour' for duration or volume." — [Bar & Bench](https://www.barandbench.com/columns/youtubers-in-peril-why-indias-fair-use-laws-need-urgent-clarification); [SpicyIP](https://spicyip.com/2021/11/aaj-tak-v-newslaundry-assessing-the-fair-dealing-question.html)
- In *Super Cassettes v Hamar Television* (Delhi HC, 2011), the court held fair dealing is a question of "fact, degree and overall impression": "a few seconds, can be the heart of it", and the user "must actually be doing criticism, review or reportage." — [Legal Republic summary](https://www.legalrepublic.in/everyday-law/youtube-pirated-copyright-takedown/) *(secondary summary)*
- SpicyIP reads the Delhi High Court as applying a four-factor test similar to the US in the *TV Today (Aaj Tak) v Newslaundry* dispute over short news clips used for critique. — [SpicyIP, 2021](https://spicyip.com/2021/11/aaj-tak-v-newslaundry-assessing-the-fair-dealing-question.html) *(commentary; the case outcome was not verified)*

**Reused content (YPP monetisation)**
- Reused content means repurposing content "without adding significant original commentary, substantive modifications, or educational or entertainment value." It "applies to your channel as a whole." It "is separate from YouTube's Copyright enforcement… not based on copyright, permission, or fair use… applies even if you have permission." — [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- Monetisable: "Using clips for a critical review", "Edited footage from other creators where you add a storyline and commentary." Not monetisable: "Clips of moments… edited together with little or no narrative", and "Content that exclusively features readings of other materials you did not originally create, like text from websites or news feeds." — [same](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- Since the 15 July 2025 rename from "repetitious" to "inauthentic content", ineligible content includes "AI-generated content made with generic or unoriginal templates giving the impression of mass production without adding the creator's original, authentic insights or perspective." Allowed: "using AI to edit your video scripts or generate a unique background visual" for "a unique, well-researched… narrative." — [same](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)
- Reviewers may look at a channel's main theme, most viewed and newest videos, metadata and About section. — [same](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)

**AI disclosure**
- Creators "must disclose GenAI content that: Makes a real person appear to say or do something they didn't do; Alters footage of a real event or place; Generates a realistic scene that didn't actually occur." Examples requiring disclosure include "Making it appear as if someone gave advice that they did not actually give." — [YouTube Help: Disclosing use of GenAI content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- No disclosure is needed for "Production assistance, like using generative AI tools to create or improve a video outline, script…", "Caption creation", "Video sharpening, upscaling or repair and voice or audio repair", or "Cloning one's own voice to create voice overs or dubs." — [same](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- "Disclosing AI content alone won't limit a video's audience or impact its eligibility to earn money." Persistent non-disclosure can lead to labels applied by YouTube, content removal or YPP suspension. YouTube may auto-label via C2PA or detection. — [same](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491?hl=en)
- Channels using "AI-generated personas" presenting as human experts giving advice on "health, legal issues, finances, or politics" are not monetisable. — [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)

**Creator practice**
- For CC BY reuse, credit "Title of the work, Author, Source (URL), License info" in the video and description, by "text overlay or voiceover." — [YouTube Help: License types](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797468?hl=en)
- CMSI: attribution "through an accompanying on-screen identification or a mention in the film's final credits." — [CMSI](https://cmsimpact.org/code/documentary-filmmakers-statement-of-best-practices-in-fair-use/)

### Inferences
- A synthetic narrator voice that is not a real person's, speaking original script over **unaltered** real clips, does not appear to be on YouTube's "must disclose" list. That is my reading: the list targets realistic depictions of real people, events or places. A **photorealistic AI presenter**, though, could arguably be a "realistic scene that didn't occur", and any AI voice cloning, lip-sync or "enhancement" that changes a real person's words clearly requires disclosure. Since disclosure doesn't reduce reach or monetisation, the low-risk default is to tick "AI use" whenever an AI presenter or narrator is used, and to say so in the description. That is consistent with BBC 3.4.29's transparency principle.
- The app's model (an AI-written narrative interspersed with 3–15 s clips) fits the "storyline and commentary" pattern YouTube allows. The risk is at channel level, if many videos look template-generated. Original research, analysis and varied structure per video are the mitigation, and that is a product and editorial issue, not a per-clip check.
- Per-clip fair use posture: use the clip for the point it illustrates; keep it as short as needed; avoid using a source's most valuable segment (for example a product reveal moment) when a different line would do; draw clips from varied sources; attribute every clip on-screen and in the description; and keep a written per-clip rationale. This mirrors the CMSI limitations, and a written rationale also helps in Content ID disputes.
- The rules differ by country: the US has an open-ended four-factor test, while the UK and India require the use to fit a listed purpose (criticism or review, or reporting current events) plus fairness. "Illustrative" historical use that isn't criticism or review may be weaker under fair dealing than under US fair use.

### Gaps
- No primary data was found on how Content ID treats short clips of Nvidia keynotes specifically, or whether Nvidia, CNBC, Bloomberg and similar channels register their uploads in Content ID. This needs empirical testing (for example private or unlisted test uploads).
- The US Copyright Office Fair Use Index and the Stanford Fair Use Center were not fetched.
- **EU AI Act Article 50** transparency obligations for AI-generated content, and India's synthetic-media labelling rules (IT Rules amendments), were not researched. Both may bear on AI narration, so legal review is needed.
- YouTube's "likeness detection" tools for public figures were not researched.
- YouTube's Fair Use Protection indemnity is "only able to offer this initiative to creators living in the United States who agree to have their videos only available in the United States" ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9783148?hl=en)). It is invitation-only and not a practical safeguard.

## 7. Licences: Creative Commons on YouTube, official channels, government footage, press kits

### Takeaway
On YouTube a video is under either the Standard YouTube licence (no reuse rights granted to other creators) or CC BY (reuse with attribution, including commercial use). Uploading to YouTube does not grant reuse rights, and YouTube can't grant them. US federal government footage made by employees as part of their official duties is generally public domain in the US, but third-party material inside it, publicity rights, and foreign use are exceptions. State and local government works can be copyrighted. Without CC BY, public domain status or explicit permission, use rests on fair use or fair dealing.

### Cited Findings
- YouTube's two licence types are the Standard YouTube licence (the default) and Creative Commons Attribution. Under CC BY, "other creators can reuse your work subject to the terms of the CC BY license," with credit. "You can monetize Creative Commons content if the license agreement grants you rights to use it commercially." A video with a Content ID claim cannot be marked CC BY. — [YouTube Help: License types](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797468?hl=en)
- "YouTube cannot grant you the rights to use content that has already been uploaded to YouTube. If you want to use someone else's YouTube content, you may want to reach out to them directly." — [YouTube Help: License types](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797468?hl=en)
- 17 U.S.C. §105: "Copyright protection under this title is not available for any work of the United States Government", meaning "a work prepared by an officer or employee of the United States Government as part of that person's official duties." — [LII, 17 U.S.C. §105](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/105)
- NARA: federal works are "treated as though they are in the public domain," but some records contain material "restricted by federal copyright law… contract law, deed restrictions, or publicity rights." NARA "does not confirm copyright status… [or] indemnify the user." — [National Archives: Permissions](https://www.archives.gov/research/motion-pictures/permissions)
- DHS: videos are public domain "unless noted", but "Copyrighted music or footage, which is incorporated in a production, may not be used unless permission is obtained", and commercial use of segments with "a recognizable person may infringe a right of publicity." — [DHS Photo, Video & Audio Use Guidelines](https://www.dhs.gov/%69%6E%64%65%78.%70%68%70/photo-video-audio-use-guidelines) *(last updated 2022)*
- Government work status "does not apply to works of state and local governments", and "U.S. copyright laws may not protect U.S. government works outside the country… The U.S. government may assert copyright outside of the United States." — [USAGov](https://www.usa.gov/government-copyright)
- The US Government work rule "does not mean… the property of the U.S. Government"; employee works made outside official duties may be copyrighted. — [LII §105 notes](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/105)

### Inferences
- For the Nvidia project, Congressional hearing footage (for example the House committee sessions on 1–2 Dec, if public) recorded by government employees would likely be US public domain. Network or C-SPAN feeds of the same event carry their own copyright. *(C-SPAN's licensing terms were not checked.)*
- Record the YouTube licence field per source video at ingest. CC BY clips can be used with auto-generated attribution. Standard-licence clips (most keynotes and news) should be marked "relies on fair use/fair dealing" and routed to the human rationale check.
- Corporate press and media kits (for example an Nvidia newsroom B-roll page) may carry permissive terms, usually limited to editorial use and sometimes excluding commercial or monetised use. The terms should be read per asset, and BBC 3.4.14 suggests labelling company-supplied material as such.

### Gaps
- Nvidia's newsroom, press-kit or media-asset terms of use were not retrieved.
- Official YouTube channel terms beyond the standard licence (for example channel-specific reuse permissions) were not researched.
- Creative Commons' own CC BY 4.0 legal code was not fetched; the attribution elements above come from YouTube's summary. Personality and publicity rights (for example India's recent celebrity personality-rights orders) were not researched.

## 8. Proposed checklist: automated checks and human review rules for the sound-bite feature

### Takeaway
The checks below turn the standards above into pipeline rules. Automated checks block or flag; a human owner makes the final meaning, fairness and rights call with the context the tool surfaces. "Block" means the clip cannot be used without changing the script or the clip. "Flag" means human sign-off is required, with a reason recorded.

### Cited Findings
- Basis for automated checks plus human oversight: "Any product that uses AI to distribute or curate content is considered to be an editorial experience and therefore subject to editorial approval and human oversight"; automated analysis needs "a sense check." — [BBC Editorial Guidelines 3.4.18, 3.4.28](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)
- The source standards for each rule are cited in sections 1–7 above (AP, PBS, NPR, BBC, Ofcom, IPSO, CMSI, YouTube Help, Verification Handbook).

### Inferences

**A. Automated checks at ingest, matching and trimming**

| # | Check | Rule | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Provenance record | Store video ID, channel ID and name, channel "official" status, upload timestamp (UTC; YouTube may show Pacific time), title, description, licence field, view of earliest duplicate. Prefer the first-party/official channel; **flag** third-party re-uploads and compilations. | Verification Handbook; BBC 3.4.11–3.4.13 |
| A2 | Earliest-upload search | Search for the same title or keywords sorted by date, and match thumbnails and frames; **flag** if an earlier or different-context upload exists. | Verification Handbook ch. 5 |
| A3 | Recording date | Extract the event and date (for example "GTC 2024 keynote") from metadata, separate from the upload date. **Block** if no recording date can be established. | NPR (circumstances, time); BBC 3.4.32 |
| A4 | Single contiguous span | The clip is one continuous time range from one source with no internal cuts. **Block** joins by default; if allowed, require a visible transition (flash or wipe) plus human approval, never a seamless join under B-roll. | AP (no internal edits); PBS; Panorama case |
| A5 | Sentence-boundary snap | Snap in and out points to sentence or clause boundaries and natural pauses, not just word boundaries. **Flag** clips starting mid-sentence or ending before sentence end. | PBS (subject-verb-object intact) |
| A6 | Turn and qualifier scan | Scan about 2–3 sentences after the out-point and before the in-point for contrastive or qualifying markers ("but", "however", "not", "unless", "I was wrong", "that's when I realised", "of course that's not true"). **Flag** with the context shown. | Sherrod case |
| A7 | Voice and attribution scan | Detect reported speech or hypotheticals in or around the clip ("people say", "critics claim", "imagine if", "the old me thought", quoting someone else), plus sarcasm or laughter cues. **Flag.** | Sherrod; NPR "true to meaning" |
| A8 | Question capture | If the clip is an answer (interview or Q&A), capture the question and store it with the clip. **Flag** if the script implies the answer was to a different question. | NPR ("what that person was asked") |
| A9 | Context window and meaning check | Store at least ±60 s of transcript. Run a model comparison of "excerpt meaning" against "excerpt plus context". **Flag** if they diverge. Output is advisory, shown to the reviewer. | PBS full-footage test; BBC 3.4.19 |
| A10 | Quote fidelity class | Compare the script quote with the clip transcript after normalising fillers. Exact means a direct quote is OK. Near means rewrite the script to the exact words. Paraphrase means narration must read as paraphrase (no quote marks, "has argued that…"). Mismatch means **block**. | AP; NPR Honesty |
| A11 | Transcript confidence | Confirm the matched words with a second ASR pass or the original creator captions. **Flag** low-confidence words; on-screen captions must be verbatim, with "[inaudible]" where needed. | AP (accuracy); Verification Handbook (body of evidence) |
| A12 | Speaker identity | Diarisation shows a single speaker for the matched words; the named person's face is on screen while speaking; a voice embedding matches a reference from a verified official source. **Flag** mismatches, other speakers, or no face. | BBC 3.4.6; Verification Handbook |
| A13 | Dub, translation and reader detection | Use the original audio track only (reject YouTube alternate or auto-dubbed tracks); detect interpreter voice-over and language mismatch; detect a host or reporter reading the person's quote. **Block** dubbed audio; translations must be labelled. | AP (translate faithfully, note language) |
| A14 | Synthetic and parody signals | **Flag** sources with a YouTube "altered or synthetic" label, C2PA AI assertions, or titles or descriptions containing "AI", "deepfake", "parody", "fan-made" or "voice", and channels with no history. | YouTube GenAI policy; Verification Handbook 3 |
| A15 | No alteration of real speech | Hard rule: no speed or pitch change, no word-level audio edits, no AI voice cloning, lip-sync or "fill-in" of a real person. Audio clean-up limited to non-word noise. | BBC 3.4.26; YouTube disclosure list |
| A16 | Duration and proportion | Each clip is 3–15 s and no longer than the illustrated point needs. Track total seconds per source video and per channel per project; **flag** heavy reliance on one source. | CMSI limitations |
| A17 | Licence routing | CC BY means auto-attribute (title, author, URL, licence). US federal government source means note public-domain status but scan for embedded third-party material. Standard licence means fair use/fair dealing reliance, routed to H6. | YouTube licence types; 17 USC §105; NARA |
| A18 | Auto labels and credits | Generate a lower-third with name, role-at-the-time, venue and date (for example "Jensen Huang, Nvidia CEO — GTC keynote, Mar 2024"), and an "ARCHIVE" tag when the recording predates the events being narrated (or is older than a set age). Generate a description credit with a deep link (`&t=`) for each clip. | BBC 3.4.32; CMSI attribution; YouTube CC credit |
| A19 | Temporal-implication guard | **Flag** any clip recorded **before** an event that is placed directly after narration about that event or reactions to it (for example a 2024 clip after "after the resignation, Huang…"). Requires the archive label plus human approval. | BBC 3.4.32; NPR; Ofcom 7.8 |
| A20 | Sensitive-segment juxtaposition guard | Tag script sentences containing allegation, scandal, investigation, resignation, spy or affair terms. Any clip within N seconds (for example 20 s) of a tagged sentence needs the mandatory human check H4; clips whose words could read as admission or response ("mistake", "sorry", "trust", "I take responsibility", and similar) are **blocked** unless they are actually about the allegation. | BBC 3.4.25; Panorama B-roll; Ofcom 7.9 |
| A21 | Role-at-the-time | Check that the title shown matches the person's role at the recording date (for example "CEO" before 12 Nov 2026, "former CEO" after). | BBC 3.4.33; accuracy |
| A22 | AI disclosure flag | If the video uses an AI narrator or presenter, add a description note ("Narration is AI-generated; clips are unaltered recordings of the people shown"). If an AI presenter is photorealistic, or any real-person footage was AI-altered, set YouTube "AI use = Yes". | YouTube GenAI policy; BBC 3.4.29 |
| A23 | Freshness re-check | Before publishing, re-query news for each named person and topic since script lock; **flag** new developments (for example the Huang reinstatement push, the 1–2 Dec 2026 House sessions). Add an "Information as of [date]" card. | BBC 3.4.33 |

**B. Human review: the owner signs off per clip and per video**

| # | Review step | What the reviewer confirms |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | Watch in context | Watch the clip with at least 30–60 s either side (or the full answer or story). Confirm the meaning is preserved, the clip doesn't cut before a "turn", and the speaker would recognise their meaning. Record "pass" with a note. (PBS; public media guide; Sherrod) |
| H2 | Identity and source | Confirm the person is who the label says, the channel is original or official (or explain why not), and nothing suggests parody, impersonation, dubbing or synthetic media. (Verification Handbook) |
| H3 | Quote wording | Where the script uses quotation marks or "said", check it word for word against the audio. Where it paraphrases, check the narration reads as paraphrase and is faithful. (AP; NPR) |
| H4 | Allegation segments | Check that (a) facts, allegations and denials are clearly separated and attributed to named outlets; (b) "not independently confirmed" caveats are carried where sources use them; (c) the subject's response (for example Huang's published statement, the board statement) is included; (d) no clip next to an allegation implies an admission or response; (e) any new claim has a request for comment logged; (f) legal review for anything beyond widely reported facts. (Ofcom 7.9/7.11/7.13; AP; BBC 3.4.22; IPSO 1) |
| H5 | Sequence watch-through | Watch the full assembled video once for juxtaposition, music and B-roll effects that create implications (the Panorama B-roll problem), and for any clip that implies recency. (BBC 3.4.25) |
| H6 | Rights rationale | For Standard-licence clips, record a one-line fair use/fair dealing rationale: what point the clip illustrates or is critiqued for, why this length, and that it is not the source's "heart" or just a substitute for shooting footage. Note target territories (US fair use versus UK/India fair dealing purposes). Consult a lawyer for high-value or monetised uses. (CMSI; GOV.UK; Super Cassettes) |
| H7 | Channel-level originality | Confirm each video carries substantive original research, analysis and narrative, and that the channel doesn't read as templated or mass-produced. (YouTube reused and inauthentic content policies) |
| H8 | Corrections | Keep a corrections log. If an error is found after publishing, fix or re-upload, and add a visible correction note (pinned comment or description) saying what was wrong. (BBC 3.4.34–3.4.36; IPSO 1(ii)) |

**C. Data the app should store per clip (audit trail)**
Source URL and video ID; channel; licence; upload date and recording date; in and out timestamps; verbatim transcript of the clip and context window; question (if any); fidelity class; speaker-check results; flags raised and how they were resolved; reviewer name, timestamp and notes; fair use rationale; generated label and credit text. BBC 3.4.15 requires research records kept "in a way that allows double-checking" ([BBC](https://www.bbc.com/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)), and a written rationale supports Content ID disputes.

### Gaps
- Thresholds (context-window length, juxtaposition distance, "archive" age, word-match percentage) are product choices; no standard gives numbers.
- The reliability of automated meaning-change detection (LLM classifiers) for excerpts was not researched. Treat its output as advisory only.
- None of this has been reviewed by a lawyer. Copyright, defamation, publicity-rights and AI-labelling obligations vary by country (US, UK, EU, India) and should be confirmed before monetised publication, especially for the Huang allegation segment.
