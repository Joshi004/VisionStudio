# Sound bite editing in narrated explainers: patterns for bringing in a real person's clip

Scope note for the report writer. Two kinds of evidence are kept apart throughout:
- **Sources say**: journalism and editing guides, academic studies, and creator interviews. Each is cited inline.
- **Observed**: my own reading of the timestamped caption transcripts of specific YouTube videos, which I downloaded with `youtube-transcript-api`. Each is marked "Observed" and links to the video. **Limitation:** I worked from captions, not the picture. Where one speaker hands over to another, I inferred it from changes in wording, caption tags such as `[Johnny]` or `>>`, and first-person speech. I could not see lower thirds, quote cards, or whether a clip was muted. All timestamps are approximate (±1–2 s).

Several 2026 videos analysed here (CNBC, Bloomberg Originals, ColdFusion) cover news about Jensen Huang that I have not verified. I use them only for their editing structure, not as sources on those events.

## Q1. What are the distinct patterns for bringing in a sound bite?

### Takeaway
NPR's training toolbox names more than a dozen ways to write into and out of tape. Broadcast TV calls its default unit the VO-SOT: the narrator speaks, **stops**, and the bite plays in full. In nearly every documented pattern the narrator is **silent while the real person speaks**. What changes from pattern to pattern is what the narrator says *before* the clip (introduce the speaker, paraphrase the point, start a sentence, ask a question, or nothing at all) and *after* it (react, echo, take the next step, or nothing). The one pattern where the narrator talks over the person ("lip flap") is discouraged in TV news.

### Cited Findings

**The basic broadcast unit (VO-SOT)**
- "VOSOT ... stands for voice-over sound on tape, meaning the anchor reads a voice-over portion, then stops talking while a sound bite is heard full. After the SOT, it may return to more voice-over (VO-SOT-VO), return to an anchor for a tag, or go directly on to the next story." — [Writing for Electronic Media (UEN Pressbooks), VOSOTs chapter](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/writingforelectronicmedia/chapter/vosots/)
- The same textbook advises choosing the bite *before* writing the narration: "It is usually easier to do this if you choose the sound bite first ... you can artfully craft your script to lead right into it. You want the SOT to fit right after the anchor's last words." — [UEN Pressbooks](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/writingforelectronicmedia/chapter/vosots/)
- A school package-scripting guide says the same: "Place the selected bites into the script before writing most of the narration ... Type the source's exact words into the audio column ... [to] avoid writing narration that repeats it." — [Bellville AV, Section 4: Write the Video Package Script](https://bellvilleav.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Section-4-Write-the-Video-Package-Script.pdf)
- A broadcast script marks each bite with its duration and **out-cue** (the last 3–4 words spoken) so the director knows when to cut back. — [How to Write a News Report Script for TV/Broadcast (YouTube tutorial)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2xGvoem04Do)

**NPR Training's toolbox (Alison MacAdam, NPR's audio storytelling specialist 2015–2018).** This is the most complete published taxonomy I found. Direct paraphrases with examples — [NPR Training: Writing through sound: a toolbox for getting into and out of your tape](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-66787/writing-through-sound-a-toolbox-for-getting-into-and-out-of-your-tape):
- Default: "Person X, who has XX job, says Y about Thing Z." NPR's verdict: "This approach is OK, and sometimes it's the best choice, but... it's boring. Especially if it's the only way you introduce tape."
- *Getting into tape:*
  1. **Tease + back ID**: the tape plays first, and the narrator identifies the speaker afterwards. "Teases and back ID's work best with a relatively short cut of tease tape (<10 secs), so that the listener doesn't have time to start wondering who's speaking." Uses: establishing a character, making a key point without interrupting the flow, getting out of the way in dramatic moments. Caution: it confuses the listener if the tape isn't catchy or the voice sounds like other voices in the piece.
  2. **The Hint**: an inflection, or a word such as "technically", signals what is coming.
  3. **Question, then answer**: the narrator asks a question and the bite answers it ("So was this the glorious return of regular order?" followed by "You could call it what you want...").
  4. **Signpost**: "Listen to this...", "This is the sound of...", "Which brings us to...".
  5. **Set-up (general), then explanation (specific)**: "His mother, Diane Foley, told ABC the U.S. government offered little in the way of information or support." followed by "We had to beg. We were an annoyance...".
  6. **Begin a thought and let the tape complete it**: "Eighteen-year-old Alicia Henry arrived at Scavo a year ago as a sophomore behind in credit. Now..." followed by "I could have graduated this year, but I chose not to."
- *Getting out of tape:*
  1. **"That is the sound of..."** after a montage.
  2. **React**: "Sometimes tape demands a human response. The audio is angry or sad or profound. Just moving on to another thought would be too abrupt."
  3. **Echo**: the narrator repeats a word from the tape and builds on it. The tape ends "...and are outspoken about it." and the narrator continues "Outspoken, he says, in languages other than English, which is why you might not be hearing them."
  4. **Take the next step**: the narrator expands on what was just said ("Just much more intense.").
  5. **Emphasize/repeat**: "Tape whizzes past listeners' ears ... If a reporter wants to make sure the listener heard something or underline its importance (or its absurdity), he/she can repeat the tape or add emphasis." Example: "Yeah, there is a drought shame app."
  6. **Complete the thought or sentence**: the tape ends mid-idea and the narrator picks it up: "Because, Dominik Flammer says, ..."
- *Other uses:* **Montage/waterfall** ("great way to demonstrate variety or sameness of opinions, repetition"). **Keep your questions in** (the interviewer is heard). **Butt cut** (one bite directly after another, "great for indicating contrast, transitions, or agreement," with the same caution as tease tape). **Repeat your tape** (play the clip again so the audience hears it closely).

**Other placement guidance**
- A radio lead-in "previews the sound bite" and is followed after the bite by "a quick outro with any details that weren't featured in the audio." — [CBI "Writing for the Ear" (WSOU-FM / 1010 WINS), 2019](https://www.askcbi.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Writing-for-the-Ear-NSEMC-2019-FINAL-1.pdf)
- Apple's documentary teaching guide describes a "typical setup for documentaries and news stories" as "news, news, reaction to that news, news, reaction, news, reaction, news, news, reaction". It adds: "You can place two interviews back-to-back, but make sure they don't repeat; instead, they should complement each other." — [Apple Education, Documentary Films guide](https://www.apple.com/education/docs/documentary_guide_12-11.pdf)
- **Cold open.** "Most documentary scripts open with a cold open: a moment from the middle of the story, then the title, then the beginning." The same source measured title cards landing at 52 s (Veritasium), 89 s (Linus Tech Tips), 112 s (MKBHD) and 150 s (Johnny Harris) after a cold open. This is a vendor blog with its own measurements and moderate reliability. — [Umbrella Creators, documentary editing guide](https://umbrellacreators.com/blog/how-to-edit-documentary-youtube-videos); [Umbrella Creators, YouTube hooks: the first 30 seconds](https://umbrellacreators.com/blog/the-30-second-rule-engineering-youtube-hooks)
- Documentary openings are often "a montage of conflict – a series of images from the films highest point of conflict." Example: *Going Clear* (2015), where questions from a Scientology audit are answered by sound bites from later in the film. — [Brendon Marotta, How To Structure Issue Documentary Openings](https://brendonmarotta.com/247/documentary-openings/)
- **Lip flap (muted clip under narration) is discouraged.** "Lip flap happens when the candidate is shown in formal talking mode ... so that viewers clearly see the candidate's mouth moving but what he is saying is not heard as primary sound ... the reporter's voice-over is heard while the candidate is shown in speech mode ... Generally, instances of lip flap are viewed as unflattering to candidates and are discouraged in industry circles ... (b-roll of related events is preferred instead)." The same paper defines a **"sound up"**: "a very short (2–3 seconds) sound bite of a candidate talking—but not in formal speech-mode." — [Bucy & Grabe (2007), "Taking Television Seriously", Journal of Communication 57, via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286745582_Image_Bite_Politics_News_and_the_Visual_Framing_of_Elections)
- **Quote cards** (the narrator reads exact words that also appear on screen) are an established YouTube documentary device when no audio exists or the source is written. One faceless documentary channel's style guide: "Quotes go up as on-screen quote cards, so the viewer reads the words as well as hearing them." These sources are vendor blogs and lower-authority, but they are consistent with each other. — [Umbrella Creators, Tenerife case study](https://umbrellacreators.com/case-studies/interpreter-tenerife-documentary-case-study); [Moshion, animated text guide](https://moshion.app/resources/animated-text-motion-graphics) (names PolyMatter and TLDR News as users of pull quotes)

### Pattern catalogue with timeline implications
The pattern names are mine. They synthesise the cited sources and the videos I observed (see Q3 for timestamps). The column "Narrator during clip" is what an automated timeline must do.

### Inferences
- In every endorsed pattern the narrator **stops** for the clip. The only overlap pattern (P12) is discouraged. For an automated timeline, the default should be: narration audio goes silent, or drops to room tone or music, for the full clip duration.
- The owner's "replace" idea is, in effect, **P1 (attributed lead-in)**. The narrator's sentence "He once told Stanford students, '[quote]'" becomes "He once told Stanford students," followed by the clip. That is the textbook-standard pattern, not an odd one, provided:
  (a) the lead-in left after removing the quote still reads as a complete set-up, with no dangling "that" and no paraphrase that pre-states the quote; and
  (b) the clip starts cleanly on or just before the quoted words.
- **Real speech is messier than the scripted quote.** The scripted line "I wish upon you ample doses of pain and suffering" is preceded in the real 2024 clip by "...and so if I could wish upon you I don't know how to do it for all of you Stanford students, I wish upon you ample doses of pain and suffering" ([YouTube excerpt transcript](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vOvQSqY7Jgc)). The cut points must be chosen on the real audio, not the script text, or the clip will start mid-false-start.
- "Replace" in its current form covers only P1 (and P11 as the fallback when no clip is found). P2, P4, P6, P7, P8 and P9 all need the script to be written *around* the clip, not just have the clip swapped in. This supports the textbook advice to choose the bite first and write to it.

### Gaps
- I found no published frequency count of how often each pattern is used across YouTube explainers. The relative frequencies in Q3 and Q4 come from my small sample of transcripts only.
- Captions can't show whether a clip was muted with subtitles (P12) or shown as a quote card over narration (P11). I could not verify these visually in the sampled videos.

## Q2. Should the narrator avoid saying the exact quoted words (redundancy) or say them first (reinforcement)?

### Takeaway
Broadcast and radio writing guides agree: **do not repeat the bite's words in the lead-in** ("parroting"). Preview who is speaking and the idea or emotion, in different words. Repetition is endorsed only in specific forms: **after** the clip (echo, emphasis, repeating hard-to-hear tape), or when the tape is murky. Big YouTube creators do not always follow the rule. I saw both a ColdFusion video and a Johnny Harris video say the line (or its gist) and then play it.

### Cited Findings
- "You also don't want the bite to repeat what the anchor just said." Bad: "FARMER BROWN SAYS HE CAN'T BELIEVE HIS COW GOT OUT," followed by "I just can't believe the cow would get out like this..." Better: "FARMER BROWN SAYS HE'S SURPRISED HIS COW IS GONE." — [UEN Pressbooks, VOSOTs](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/writingforelectronicmedia/chapter/vosots/)
- "You don't want to give away the contents of your sound bite in your preview. Don't use the same phrasing in your intro that's used in the bite." — [CBI "Writing for the Ear"](https://www.askcbi.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Writing-for-the-Ear-NSEMC-2019-FINAL-1.pdf)
- A lead-in should "introduce the speaker and give context without parroting—that means repeating exactly what the speaker is about to say ... This makes the audience feel like they're hearing double." Example of the mistake: "Sarah says she worked very hard on this." followed by "I worked really, really hard on this." — [YouTube broadcast-script tutorial](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2xGvoem04Do)
- "Neither sentence [setup or exit] should repeat the bite word for word ... Do not write, She had this to say, or He tells us more." Weak: "VO: Coach Ramirez says the team must improve communication." followed by "SOT: We need to improve our communication." — [Bellville AV package-scripting guide](https://bellvilleav.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Section-4-Write-the-Video-Package-Script.pdf)
- From Alex Chadwick's NPR guidance: "It's not so useful to hear a reporter state that the Mayor said he's going to run for re-election, and then hear a piece of tape with Mayor saying: 'I'm going to run for re-election.' Could the reporter tell us instead that this is an expected announcement, or a surprise? Did the mayor sound confident, or worried?" — [Transom, Alex Chadwick](https://transom.org/2015/alex-chadwick/)
- Course notes: "Make sure your transitions don't repeat the sound bites" and "Avoid repetition especially between the reporter's writing and the sound Bite." — [AAST Broadcast News Writing course notes (PDF)](https://aast.edu/pheed/staffadminview/pdf_retreive.php?stafftype=staffcourses&url=231_97525_MD313_2013_1__2_1_Broadcast+News+Writing.pdf)
- **Where repetition is endorsed:** NPR's "echo" and "emphasize/repeat" techniques come *after* the tape ([NPR Training toolbox](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-66787/writing-through-sound-a-toolbox-for-getting-into-and-out-of-your-tape)). An NPR editor on murky tape: "I'm a big believer in restating key phrases from murky actualities ... people are distracted. We need to point out the important stuff." — [NPR Storytelling (Tumblr)](https://nprstorytelling.tumblr.com/post/118461686173/just-because-your-tape-is-hard-to-hear-doesnt)
- Noah Adams (NPR) calls accidental "echoes" "the single greatest easily corrected writer's mistake": a repeated word or phrase that becomes noticeable. This is about *unintentional* repetition. — [Transom, Alex Chadwick](https://transom.org/2015/alex-chadwick/)
- **Observed: creators who repeat before the clip anyway.**
  - ColdFusion, *The Meteoric Rise of Nvidia* (2023), 27:59–28:28. The narrator paraphrases at length: Huang "stated in an interview that if he was allowed to go back in time he would choose to not relive this career path ... in his view nobody in their right mind would want to endure that pressure." Then a ~67 s clip plays, including the interviewer's question, in which Huang says "I wouldn't do it ... nobody in their right mind would do it." The narrator pre-states both the conclusion and the exact phrase. — [ColdFusion video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nVtb2vNUOdU)
  - Johnny Harris, *The Problem With Elon Musk*, 03:12. Narrator: "At the age of 12, he invented a video game." Clip: "Mom, do you remember when I was 12 and I created my own video game." Then narrator: "That he sold to a magazine for," and clip: "$500." This is near-verbatim repetition used deliberately, as a fast ping-pong. — [Johnny Harris video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WYQxG4KEzvo)
  - Same video, 01:46. Narrator: "he was bullied pretty relentlessly by kids at school." Clip: "You know, bullied quite a lot. I got punched in the face many times." One keyword is echoed, and the clip adds new specific detail ("punched in the face"). — [Johnny Harris video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WYQxG4KEzvo)

### Inferences
- When pre-stating works on YouTube, the clip seems to add something the narration didn't: a vivid detail, emotion, or proof ("it's really him"). In the Harris examples, repetition is kept to 1–3 s bites at a fast pace, so the doubling reads as confirmation rather than a lag. The long ColdFusion clip after a full paraphrase is the riskier form: the viewer already has the information for over a minute of tape. I could not measure audience reaction, such as retention dips.
- For the app, a safe default that follows the textbooks: the narrator's lead-in states **who / where / when and why it matters**, never the quoted words. The clip carries the exact words. This is what "replace" produces automatically, *if* the script's lead-in sentence doesn't also paraphrase the quote.
- Repetition can be offered as an explicit, opt-in *after-clip* move (echo or emphasis: "Ample doses of pain and suffering. He meant it."), or when clip audio quality is poor. It shouldn't be a before-clip default.
- Business/history explainers often use a famous line partly as "proof it's real". There, a brief paraphrase-then-clip (P2) is defensible if the lead-in uses different words, e.g., "He told Stanford's graduates he hoped they would suffer." This matches the textbook advice: preview the idea, not the phrasing.

### Gaps
- I found no first-person statement from a YouTube explainer creator (Harris, Vox, ColdFusion and others) specifically about redundancy between narration and bites. Their practice above is observed, not stated.
- No audience-research data on redundancy versus reinforcement for video sound bites. Classic dual-coding / redundancy research in multimedia learning may be relevant but was not researched here.

## Q3. How do successful explainer channels actually do it? (concrete moments)

### Takeaway
Clip-led docs (CNBC, Bloomberg Originals, Johnny Harris) open with a cold-open or montage of real voices. They then alternate short narration with frequent bites, mostly attributed lead-in, set-up then confirm, and sentence-completion. Narration-led channels (ColdFusion's Nvidia video, a faceless "AI-style" Jensen doc) mostly have the narrator **read** quotes and use few real clips, sometimes one long one. The same Jensen-TSMC quote shows the split: CNBC plays the clip, ColdFusion reads it aloud.

### Cited Findings
- **Johnny Harris, stated process.** He writes a two-column script. The left column is short voice-over sentences; the right column codes visuals, including specific archive ("we need archive of Musk as an 8-year-old"). For the Musk biography, the opening "visual anchors ... are moments with him, moments of him saying something. um sound bites basically." He chose a composite of two interviews ("My mind is a storm") as the first hook: "we went through probably 10 different sound bites ... I think not enough is said about picking the right clips to put into the beginning of your video." — [The Rise of Johnny Harris, Explained (interview)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fa1_VWuIElE)
- Harris: "I'm not going to say something unless I have a visual that can dance with what I'm saying." His process runs: a 3–4 week researcher brief, then a 3–4 day scripting week, then a "shame cut" (radio edit with crude visuals), then rough cut, fine cut, audio lock and picture lock. — [The Rise of Johnny Harris](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fa1_VWuIElE); [David Perell, How I Write: Johnny Harris (transcript)](https://viewrankai.com/tools/youtube-transcript/zq4b96m1AvM)

'No task is beneath me.'", "'I was a dishwasher, I was a busboy, I was a waiter,' he said.", and "his answer surprised everyone. 'I wouldn't do it.'" From captions alone I **cannot tell** whether these are the narrator's voice or real clips. "...he said" suggests narrator-read (P11). "His answer surprised everyone." followed by the quote has the structure of a P1 lead-in. — [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vMqpe_dtLnQ)
- Where the target quote appears. Jensen's "I wish upon you ample doses of pain and suffering" comes from his March 2024 keynote at the Stanford SIEPR Economic Summit ([Startup Archive](https://www.startuparchive.org/p/jensen-huang-greatness-comes-from-character)). Patrick Collison quoted it back to him in a Stripe Sessions conversation and framed it as "a Stanford event recently, at the GSB, I think." Huang then restated the idea ("I wish upon you plenty of pain and suffering"), giving a second, longer source clip ([Stripe conversation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8Pfa8kPjUio)). The venue descriptions differ slightly (SIEPR summit vs "GSB"); SIEPR is the more specific attribution. None of the sampled Nvidia explainers used this exact line. The closest was ColdFusion's "pain and suffering" passage from a different interview (29:08).

### Inferences
- I see two house styles:
  - **(A) Clip-led** (CNBC, Bloomberg, Johnny Harris): a bite every 15–40 s, cold-open montages, short bites, and many lead-in types.
  - **(B) Narration-led** (ColdFusion's Nvidia video, faceless docs): the narrator reads most quotes, and one or a few real clips are saved for an emotional peak.
  An AI-narrated product with a few 3–15 s clips per video fits style (B), with style (A)'s lead-in discipline.
- A narrator-read quote with "quote" or "he said" (P11) is the natural **fallback** when a clip can't be found or licensed. So "replace" is best seen as upgrading P11 to P1 when a clip is available. That supports keeping the quote in the script and cutting it at render time, *provided* the lead-in is written so that it works on its own.
- Repeated reuse of the same bite (the CNBC 2023 and 2026 pieces) suggests that signature lines get recycled across videos. A clip library keyed by quote is a reasonable product idea, though this is outside the research scope.

### Gaps
- I did not analyse videos from Vox, Wendover, PolyMatter, LEMMiNO, Company Man, Magnates Media, Business Casual, Modern MBA, or Netflix's *Dirty Money*. Several are mostly narration and animation with few interview clips (e.g., Wendover, PolyMatter), but I did not verify this here. No relevant Modern MBA Nvidia video appeared in search.
- Captions don't show visuals, so lower-third use, quote cards and muted-subtitle use in these videos are unverified.

## Q4. How often do sound bites appear, and how long are they?

### Takeaway
On US network TV news, the average political sound bite shrank from about 43 s (1968) to about 9 s (1988) and about 7.7–8 s by 2000–2004. Broadcast teaching norms put bites at 5–15 s, with anything near 15 s needing justification. In my small YouTube sample, clip-led docs run about 2–4 bites per minute at 1–28 s each. Narration-led docs use far fewer, sometimes one long clip.

### Cited Findings
- Hallin (1992): "the average sound bite has declined from 43 to 9 seconds" in network evening news, 1968 to 1988. He explains it as a shift to "a more mediated, journalist-centered form of journalism." — [Hallin, Journal of Communication 42 (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1992.tb00775.x)
- Hallin: in 1968 "nearly one-quarter of all sound bites were a minute or longer"; "By 1988 ... only four percent of all sound bites were as long as 20 seconds. The average was a mere 8.9 seconds." Example: a 1968 CBS story had five bites averaging 60 s; a 1988 ABC story had 10 bites averaging 8.5 s. Hallin: newsmakers' words are now "treated as raw material to be taken apart, combined with other sounds and images, and woven into a new narrative." — [Hallin, "Sound Bite Democracy," Wilson Quarterly (1992)](https://www.wilsonquarterly.com/quarterly/media-make-campaign/sound-bite-democracy)
- Share of election-story time taken up by sound bites: 17.6% (1968) down to 5.7% (1988). Adatto independently measured 42.3 s down to 9.8 s. 1992: 8.4 s; 1996: 8.2 s (Center for Media and Public Affairs). — [Blake (1998), BYU Sigma](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/sigma/vol16/iss1/4)
- Bucy & Grabe: average candidate sound bite 9.19 s (1992), 9.23 s (1996), 8.03 s (2000), 7.73 s (2004). They cite Lichter (2001) for 7.8 s in 2000. Candidates were *seen* (image bites) for 22.99 s per story on average, against being heard in sound bites for 18.59 s. — [Bucy & Grabe (2007)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286745582_Image_Bite_Politics_News_and_the_Visual_Framing_of_Elections)
- Teaching norm: "Sound bites are typically 5-15 seconds long, though the closer you get to 15, the more you must justify the added length." — [UEN Pressbooks](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/writingforelectronicmedia/chapter/vosots/). Another tutorial: "Most soundbites are eight to fifteen seconds long." — [YouTube tutorial](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2xGvoem04Do)
- Tease tape should stay under 10 s. — [NPR Training toolbox](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-66787/writing-through-sound-a-toolbox-for-getting-into-and-out-of-your-tape)
- **Observed (approximate, from captions):**
  - CNBC Nvidia (2023), first 5 minutes: about 11 bites (≈2.2 per minute), roughly 4–28 s each. The longest is Huang's ~28 s answer at 02:47–03:15. — [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d3L2uPuxOxU)
  - Johnny Harris Musk, 01:42–03:35 (≈1.9 min of body): about 7 bites (≈3.7 per minute), roughly 0.8–15 s, most of them 2–5 s. The cold open is ~13 s. — [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WYQxG4KEzvo)
  - ColdFusion Nvidia (31:49): one clearly identifiable long real-voice clip (~67 s, 28:28–29:35). Other quotes are narrator-read. I may have missed short clips, because auto-captions don't mark speakers. — [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nVtb2vNUOdU)
  - Faceless Jensen doc (6:37): 2–3 short quoted lines of ~1–4 s each. It is unclear whether these are clips (see Q3). — [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vMqpe_dtLnQ)

### Inferences
- The app's planned 3–15 s range matches broadcast norms (5–15 s) and the YouTube clips I observed. Quotes of 3–8 s are the most common in clip-led YouTube work. Anything over ~15 s should carry a story beat on its own (the ColdFusion 67 s clip is an outlier, used as a climax).
- For a narration-led AI explainer, "a few clips per video at emotional or proof moments" is closer to ColdFusion-style practice than 2–4 per minute. The right density depends on how many genuine bites exist. I found no rule here.

### Gaps
- No systematic study of sound-bite length or frequency in YouTube explainers (2022–2026) was found. The YouTube figures are my small-sample estimates.
- No post-2004 peer-reviewed update of network news bite length was found in this pass.

## Q5. How is the speaker identified?

### Takeaway
There are three mechanisms, often combined: (1) the narrator names the person in the lead-in; (2) a lower third (super or chyron) appears during the person's first bite; (3) a back ID, where the narrator names them right after a tease clip. Editing guides say a lower third should appear on the person's first substantial line, about 1–3 s after they start speaking, hold 4–6 s, and appear once per person unless the video is long.

### Cited Findings
- A super/lower third "identifies somebody on screen. Name, and a role or a reason we should listen to them." It should go up "on the person's first substantial line, not their first appearance ... Two to three seconds after they start speaking, not on their first frame ... Once per person per piece, unless the piece is long: over about fifteen minutes, or after a substantial gap, a repeat is helpful. Never over the end of a sentence that matters. The eye goes to new text on screen and stops listening for about a second." — [Addaly, Names, supers and the end of the film](https://addaly.com/learn/video-editing/name-supers-and-credits)
- "Bring it in a second or two after the person starts speaking and hold it for about 4 to 6 seconds ... Once per person." — [Cut.express, Lower thirds](https://cut.express/blog/lower-thirds) (vendor blog)
- Lower thirds are "used most often to identify a person on screen ... the person's name appears on the first line, with ... a description below that." Three-tier lower thirds may add when the video was shot, if it was not shot on the day of broadcast. — [Wikipedia, Lower third](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower-third)
- A good set-up should "Identify the source when the person has not already been introduced." — [Bellville AV guide](https://bellvilleav.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Section-4-Write-the-Video-Package-Script.pdf)
- A back ID comes after tease tape and must follow quickly so "the listener doesn't have time to start wondering who's speaking." — [NPR Training toolbox](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/29/g-s1-66787/writing-through-sound-a-toolbox-for-getting-into-and-out-of-your-tape)
- Archive should carry source and date labels: "Use lower-thirds, full-screen text cards, or narration to introduce the source: 'Archival footage from the Associated Press, 1972'." — [History Rise](https://historyrise.com/article/best-practices-for-combining-multiple-image-sources-in-documentaries/) (lower-authority blog)
- **Observed:** In CNBC 2023, Huang is named in narration before his first bite (01:25) and then given a fuller back ID after a later tease (02:22). In the Johnny Harris cold open, Musk is not named before the clip; the audience is expected to recognise him from the title and thumbnail. — [CNBC](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d3L2uPuxOxU); [Harris](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WYQxG4KEzvo)

### Inferences
- For the app: name the person in the narrator's lead-in (P1) *and* show a lower third with name plus source/date (e.g., "Jensen Huang · Stanford SIEPR, 2024"). The lower third should appear about 1–2 s into the clip and stay off the punchline words. For an AI-narrated product, the source/date line also does honesty work: it signals that this is real footage and not generated.
- For a central subject who appears many times (Jensen in an Nvidia video), use the lower third only the first time, or again after a long gap. Use a source/date tag for later clips from different events.

### Gaps
- I couldn't verify lower-third practice in the specific YouTube videos sampled, because I worked from captions only.

## Q6. What makes a sound bite feel impactful versus gimmicky? What do guides say about choosing and placing bites?

### Takeaway
Every source agrees on a division of labour: **narration carries facts, numbers, dates and context; bites carry emotion, opinion, personality, first-person experience, or "proof in their own words."** A bite feels weak when it restates facts, repeats the narration, needs lots of set-up, is listy, rambles or is noisy. YouTube creators add that the opening clip is a deliberate "hook" choice that signals the video's tone. Hook guides warn against misleading or invented quotes.

### Cited Findings
- "Listen for something the person says that can add flavor, personal insight, or emotion to your story. Someone stating facts is not a good bite unless the facts are astounding or in question ... [use the facts] to write your VO; you can probably say it more concisely." — [UEN Pressbooks](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/writingforelectronicmedia/chapter/vosots/)
- "Choose a bite that is representative of your interview." — [UEN Pressbooks](https://uen.pressbooks.pub/writingforelectronicmedia/chapter/vosots/)
- NPR's list of good soundbites: "Emotional ... Passionate ... Genuine ... Vivid ... Memorable ... Clarity ... Concise ... Relevant ... Focused." Things to avoid: "Basic facts ... Numbers ... Verbose ... Flat or boring ... Too fast ... Listy ... Too much explanation: It shouldn't take a lot of set up to make the soundbite clear ... Intrusive background sounds." Also: "Avoid soundbites that make sweeping statements, unless they're backed by facts." And: "not every soundbite is going to hit you in the gut, and sometimes we need to choose clips that are simply doing necessary work — moving the story forward, adding context, or breaking up a long stretch." — [NPR Training, Soundbite essentials (2026)](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2026/01/26/g-s1-104552/sound-bites-choose-good-audio-clips)
- "You use soundbites to provide emotion, opinion or expertise that the reporter cannot provide themselves. Never use a soundbite to tell the audience something boring." — [YouTube broadcast-script tutorial](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2xGvoem04Do)
- "Remember that the script should provide the facts and set up the sound bites, which provide the emotions and the stories." — [Apple Education Documentary guide](https://www.apple.com/education/docs/documentary_guide_12-11.pdf)
- From documentary interview practice: "you want emotion, not facts. This can include controversial opinions." Expository background questions are "exposition ... you will use in narration, not in an interview soundbite." — [New Doc Editing, documentary interview tip](https://newdocediting.com/documentary-interview-tip/)
- Questions to ask of a bite: "Does the bite make sense without hearing the interview question? Does it contribute something better said by the source than by the reporter? Is it short enough ...? Does the edit preserve the source's complete meaning? ... Does another bite already make the same point?" Also: "Never join separate pieces of an answer so they appear to be one continuous statement unless the break is clear and the meaning remains accurate." — [Bellville AV guide](https://bellvilleav.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Section-4-Write-the-Video-Package-Script.pdf)
- Hook ethics: an opening "should not use a quotation nobody said, claim certainty about private thoughts, imply that reconstruction is archive." — [Onira, A Documentary Hook Is a Contract](https://onira.studio/blog/a-documentary-hook-is-a-contract) (studio blog)
- Johnny Harris picked his opening clip for tone: it signals "I'm going to do something nuanced here ... I'm not doing a piece of propaganda." The Musk "my mind is a storm" moment was chosen because "no matter what you feel about Musk ... you can hear that moment and be like oh there's something more going on." — [The Rise of Johnny Harris, Explained](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fa1_VWuIElE)
- Hallin's critique of bite-driven journalism: short bites risk losing "the logic and character" of a speaker's argument. Longer segments "allow viewers to grasp the sense of an argument, to glimpse the logic and character of a candidate." — [Hallin, Wilson Quarterly](https://www.wilsonquarterly.com/quarterly/media-make-campaign/sound-bite-democracy)
- Lip flap (showing the person talking while you can't hear them) is "viewed as unflattering ... and discouraged." — [Bucy & Grabe (2007)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/286745582_Image_Bite_Politics_News_and_the_Visual_Framing_of_Elections)

### Inferences
- The Jensen "pain and suffering" line is a near-ideal bite by these criteria: emotional, surprising, short, first-person, and something only he could say. The real audio, though, has a disfluent run-up. Trimming must keep his meaning intact (the line follows his argument about resilience and low expectations). Per the "preserve complete meaning" rule, a lead-in that supplies that context helps: "He told Stanford graduates that high expectations kill resilience, and then..."
- Ways clip use turns gimmicky, all inferred from the guides:
  1. Clips used for facts the narrator could say.
  2. Lead-ins that parrot the clip.
  3. Clips that need long set-ups.
  4. Muted talking heads under narration.
  5. Noisy or low-quality clips with no repair (where NPR advises restating the key phrase).
  6. Misleading trims or composites presented as one continuous statement.
  7. Too many clips of the same voice back to back with no narrative function.
- For the AI-narrator product specifically, the contrast between a synthetic narrator voice and the real voice may make clips land harder as "proof". That argues for using them at moments of authenticity or emotion rather than for routine facts. This is speculative; I found no source on it.

### Gaps
- I found no source specific to AI-narrated videos mixing real clips, and no audience research on which sound-bite patterns maximise YouTube retention.
- I did not research copyright and fair-use constraints on clip length. That is outside the editorial scope here, but it will matter for 3–15 s YouTube clips.
